Native topic findings

  1. The central dispute concerns delegating judicial authority, not simply introducing AI into courts.
  2. Capacity and access arguments support experimentation, but faster production need not produce faster justice.
  3. Technical accuracy does not settle adjudicative legitimacy or control over legal development.
  4. Claims of machine impartiality face competing accounts of both human and algorithmic bias.
  5. Consent, rights of challenge and public confidence distinguish machine arbitration from imposed court adjudication.
  6. Unrepresented users cannot simply inherit safeguards designed around expert verification.
  7. Verification remains a concrete professional duty, but its feasibility and effectiveness remain contested.
  8. Existing applications and task benchmarks support bounded assistance, not demonstrated autonomous justice.
  9. Court governance must address institutional independence and contestability as well as output errors.
  10. Responsible adoption requires institutional reform and sustained human competence, not merely rules or software.
