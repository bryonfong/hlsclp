Corpus framing of this source: Foundations for Practice: The Whole Lawyer and the Character Quotient

Corpus synthesis of this source: IAALS uses a nationwide lawyer survey to distinguish which legal skills, professional competencies and personal characteristics new lawyers need immediately, which they should acquire later and which are merely advantageous. The report argues that early-career success depends on a broad “whole lawyer” profile rather than legal knowledge or intelligence alone, while presenting its findings as guidance for legal education rather than limits on curricular innovation.

Corpus source-specific finding

The study provides a large, structured evidence base on early-career lawyer requirements.

Exact returned source excerpt

With more than 24,000 responses from lawyers in all 50 states, we now have a clear picture of what lawyers need at the point they begin their legal careers.

Exact returned source excerpt

We asked respondents to rate the necessity of 147 foundations (plus two questions that allowed write-in responses); we asked fourteen questions to identify respondent demographics and practice information; we asked about the value of specialization in law school and in early practice; and we asked the respondents to identify the helpfulness of employment criteria (like law school attended, class rank, clinical experience, externships, and letters of recommendation).

Corpus source-specific finding

The analytical framework separates necessity from urgency.

Exact returned source excerpt

Necessity and Urgency of Foundations From a conceptual standpoint, the response options—“necessary in the short term,” “must be acquired over time,” “advantageous but not necessary,” and “not relevant”—can be thought of as getting at two different, but related, ideas: necessity of the foundation and urgency of the foundation.

Exact returned source excerpt

If a foundation is classified as either “necessary in the short term” or “must be acquired over time,” it is ultimately necessary at some point in time; the difference in these two options represents the degree of urgency for the new lawyer in gaining proficiency in the foundation.

Corpus source-specific finding

The central finding is that successful new lawyers need the broad capabilities of a “whole lawyer,” including a character quotient.

Exact returned source excerpt

Perhaps unsurprisingly, respondents considered an overwhelming majority (92%) of the foundations to be necessary; that is, 135 out of the 147 foundations.

Exact returned source excerpt

New lawyers need some legal skills and require intelligence— indeed, 84% of respondents indicated that intelligence was necessary right away—but competencies, and they are successful when they come to the job with a much broader blend of legal skills, characteristics that professional competencies, and characteristics that comprise the whole lawyer.

Corpus source-specific finding

Character, interpersonal conduct, communication and professionalism rank among the most urgent foundations.

Effective technology use is an immediate baseline, but broader innovation and online activity receive lower urgency ratings.

Conversely, engaging in online law-related professional activity and networking was seen by a majority (52%) as advantageous, but not necessary.

Corpus source-specific finding

Business development is comparatively less urgent for new lawyers.

Exact returned source excerpt

Business Development and Relations Of the seven items in the Business Development and Relations category12, the ability to retain existing business was seen as the most important foundation to have right out of law school by a fairly wide margin (38%; the next highest proportion being 17%).

Exact returned source excerpt

All but two of the foundations in this category—having an entrepreneurial mindset (45%) and engaging in marketing or fundraising activities (44%)—were seen by a majority of respondents as necessary either in the short term or to be acquired over time.
