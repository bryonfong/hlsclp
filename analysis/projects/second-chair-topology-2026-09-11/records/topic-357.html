Native topic findings

  1. The central dispute is whether cheaper legal assistance produces effective access to justice, rather than merely more legal text and court filings.
  2. Federal filing studies document rising self-representation after public GenAI release, but do not establish that AI caused it.
  3. Outcome evidence does not show a general improvement in litigants’ success, but the studies test different comparisons.
  4. Cheap drafting may shift screening costs onto courts and opponents without eliminating the need for legal judgment.
  5. Distributional concerns extend beyond unequal tool access to AI-enabled claims against disadvantaged defendants.
  6. Experimental task gains support assistance, but do not establish that lay users can replace counsel.
  7. Legal-aid evidence favors supported adoption, without yet demonstrating a reduction in unmet legal need.
  8. Court-side assistance has measured promise, but this evidence supports human augmentation rather than autonomous adjudication.
  9. Verification is both a continuing obligation and an access problem; disagreement centers on how to make it feasible.
  10. Regulatory reform proposals seek to enable guided consumer help while retaining protection against unreliable services.
  11. The practical agenda combines procedural redesign with evaluation of comprehension, fairness and remedies—not throughput alone.
