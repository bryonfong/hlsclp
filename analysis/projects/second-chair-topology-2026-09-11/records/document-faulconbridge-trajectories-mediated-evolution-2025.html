Corpus framing of this source: Machine learning changes legal work through professionally mediated evolution

Corpus synthesis of this source: James Faulconbridge’s 2025 article combines research on law-firm adoption with prior literature to argue that machine learning meaningfully reshapes legal tasks and advisory roles, but does not dictate a uniform or inevitable transformation. Professional autonomy, partnership governance, identities and technological risks shape the pace and direction of change.

Corpus source-specific finding

The central contribution is a framework of mediated evolution, not a prediction of inevitable lawyer replacement.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is argued that there is and will be mediated evolution – this being change that is moderated and given direction by interactions between the characteristics and possibilities of ML and the institutions, priorities and particularities of the legal profession.

Exact returned source excerpt

Mediated evolution is a way of conceptualising change in legal work that is material and meaningful, but which is also path dependent and non-linear and thus needs to be understood through situated analysis of the enactment in practice of change.

Corpus source-specific finding

The empirical foundation concerns actual adoption, primarily in corporate-focused firms.

The analysis below is based on insights from both primary and secondary data sources.

Open topic: Work redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Corpus framing of this source: Machine learning drives a mediated evolution of legal work

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source combines empirical research with UK law firms and prior scholarship to argue that machine learning changes legal work through intertwined automation and augmentation, rather than wholesale lawyer substitution. Its concept of “mediated evolution” explains why professional autonomy, identity, relational expertise, partnership governance, data constraints and opaque model behaviour produce selective, uneven and slower adoption than technological capability alone would suggest.

Corpus source-specific finding

ML automates bounded tasks while augmenting lawyers’ higher-value work.

Exact returned source excerpt

Automation implies “machines take over a human task” whereas augmentation implies “humans collaborate closely with machines to perform a task” (Raisch and Krakowski, 2021).

Exact returned source excerpt

Whilst then, there is evidence of algorithms replacing human tasks, when ML is used, the algorithms only replace certain tasks and hence collaboration with lawyers is needed to deliver advice to clients.

Corpus source-specific finding

ML can expand diagnosis and move lawyers toward anticipatory advice.

Exact returned source excerpt

First, lawyers can use the ability of ML to identify patterns in large document datasets to not only find important information but also to predict the likely impacts of the patterns detected, such as the value of compensation in an insurance litigation or the risks or opportunities associated with a set of contractual obligations.

Exact returned source excerpt

Second, when using ML lawyers can transform their role from problem solver to problem anticipator and preventer.

Corpus source-specific finding

ML changes the underlying basis and outputs of legal analysis.

Exact returned source excerpt

The analyses that ML conducts transform documents into statistical patterns and probabilities.

Exact returned source excerpt

ML uses patterns identified in datasets to make determinations and predictions in ways quite different to a lawyer reading and summarising.

Corpus source-specific finding

Human relational expertise limits substitution and sustains professional value.

Exact returned source excerpt

Indeed, Pakarinen and Huising (2023) describe “relational expertise” as the basis for the continuation of some key aspects of the role of human professionals despite MLs rapid and ongoing progression.

Exact returned source excerpt

For lawyers this can mean understanding the particularities of a local court or even judge and the implications for a case, the likely response of a counterparty, how an action might interact with particular circumstances faced by the client, and an array of other relational influences on decision about the right response to any diagnosis and inference work.

Corpus source-specific finding

Professional autonomy, identity and partnership governance mediate adoption.

Exact returned source excerpt

In particular, a key characteristic of lawyers as professionals is their exertion of high degrees of autonomy in their work (see for example, Faulconbridge and Muzio, 2008; Freidson, 2001), this meaning that change is negotiated rather than imposed and partnerships are often conservative and slow to change (Hinings et al., 2018).

Exact returned source excerpt

These tasks are an institutionalised part of professional identities and occupational affiliations, being tied to processes of learning (Beane, 2019) and the foundations for a lawyer’s ability to reassure that all necessary information has been analysed and considered when making decisions and advising clients.

ML requires access to large datasets that are organised and curated in a fashion that allows supervised and unsupervised learning.
