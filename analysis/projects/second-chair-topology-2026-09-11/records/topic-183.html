Native topic findings

  1. Both papers locate AI accountability in auditable organizational conduct, not model performance alone.
  2. The limits of complete assurance do not establish that practical safety testing is futile.
  3. Lifecycle records should connect risk assessment to decisions, remediation and continuing oversight.
  4. Audit standards must address social impact, not simply technical reliability or checklist compliance.
  5. Internal access improves auditability, but independence and evidence integrity remain unresolved risks.
  6. The legal-policy proposal would exchange liability benefits for substantive transparency.
  7. The cluster offers governance frameworks, not demonstrated evidence that those frameworks reduce AI harm.

AI Governance and the Verification Gap: A Framework for Law and Policy Under Computational Intractability

Academia & research · Adoption at scale (2025)

Corpus framing of this source: Governing AI through auditable processes under persistent assurance limits

Corpus synthesis of this source: AI Governance and the Verification Gap: A Framework for Law and Policy Under Computational Intractability argues that limits on comprehensive model assurance create parallel problems for liability, oversight and enterprise risk. It proposes complementary technical testing, enforceable process audits, risk-tiered liability and research into meaningful human oversight. Its contribution is a claimed formal justification for process governance, not the invention of that approach.

The paper connects computational limits to legal and evidentiary barriers.

Exact returned source excerpt

This paper’s central contribution is establishing the formal connection between proofs of computational intractability and the fundamental challenges they create for legal liability, regulatory oversight, and both external and internal corporate risk management, showing how the technical inability to empirically audit the internal safety of AI models creates a parallel evidentiary gap.

The contribution of this paper is not the invention of process-based governance, but the provision of a new and more robust formal justification for why, in the face of computational intractability, it represents a rational and necessary path forward.
