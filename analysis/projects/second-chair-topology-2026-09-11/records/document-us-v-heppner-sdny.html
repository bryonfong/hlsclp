Builds onAI privilege: Legal subject matter is insufficient

Open topic: AI privilege: Legal subject matter is insufficient

Corpus framing of this source: Heppner: privilege risks from client-directed use of public AI

Corpus synthesis of this source: The supplied Debevoise and Covington accounts explain why Judge Rakoff denied privilege and work-product protection for Heppner’s independently generated AI defense materials. They distinguish the court’s fact-specific ruling from recommendations on confidential enterprise tools, counsel-directed work and corporate AI governance.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court denied both protections for defense materials generated independently with a public AI tool.

Exact returned source excerpt

Defendant Bradley Heppner was arrested on charges of securities and wire fraud on November 4, 2025.[[1]](#_ftn1) During the search of his mansion, federal agents seized electronic devices containing approximately thirty-one documents generated using Anthropic’s AI tool Claude.[[2]](#_ftn2) After he received a grand jury subpoena and had engaged legal counsel, Heppner used Claude to prepare reports outlining his defense strategy and potential legal arguments.[[3]](#_ftn3) While Judge Rakoff has not yet issued a written opinion, the decision has significant implications for protecting client communications that involve the use of AI tools.

Exact returned source excerpt

Background** After the arrest and seizure of Heppner’s electronic devices, Heppner’s counsel claimed privilege over the Claude documents, describing them on a privilege log as “[a]rtificial intelligence-generated analysis conveying facts to counsel for [the] purpose of obtaining legal advice.”[[4]](#_ftn4) Defense counsel asserted that the AI reports were a means of consolidating Heppner’s thoughts for the express purpose of communicating with counsel.[[5]](#_ftn5) However, Heppner’s counsel conceded that Heppner prepared the AI documents on his own initiative, not at his counsel’s direction.[[6]](#_ftn6)

Corpus source-specific finding

The parties disputed whether preparing materials for counsel could protect independent AI research.

Exact returned source excerpt

Heppner asserted privilege and attorney work product over the materials, arguing that he had (1) input into the platform information that he had learned from counsel; (2) created the AI communications for the purpose of speaking with counsel to obtain legal advice; and (3) subsequently shared the AI communications with counsel for the purpose of  obtaining legal advice.

Exact returned source excerpt

The government moved for a ruling that the documents were not protected by privilege, arguing that the AI-generated documents were not confidential and not created for the purpose of obtaining legal advice.[[7]](#_ftn7) The government further contended that the defendant could not retroactively cloak unprivileged documents by later transmitting them to counsel.[[8]](#_ftn8) In addition, the government argued that the work product doctrine did not apply because the materials were prepared by the defendant on his own initiative and the doctrine “does not protect a layperson’s independent internet research.”[[9]](#_ftn9) During a pre-trial conference, Heppner’s counsel from Quinn Emanuel argued that the documents were protected by the attorney-client privilege because they incorporated information conveyed to their client during the course of the representation.[[10]](#_ftn10) With respect to the work product doctrine, Heppner’s counsel argued that a report created by a client in anticipation of litigation should be protected even if it was not done at the direction of legal counsel.[[11]](#_ftn11) **The Court’s Decision** Judge Rakoff issued an oral ruling that neither the attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine protected the AI-generated documents.[[12]](#_ftn12) The decision rests on traditional principles of privilege.

Corpus source-specific finding

The privilege analysis turned on attorney involvement and confidentiality, not merely AI authorship.

Exact returned source excerpt

Not Communications with Counsel – The court held that the AI communications were not between Heppner and his counsel because the AI tool was not an attorney, and Heppner was consulting the tool entirely on his own (*i.e.*, not at the direction of counsel).

Exact returned source excerpt

- Not Confidential – The AI communications were not confidential because Heppner communicated with a public version of a third-party AI platform, and its privacy policy did not support a reasonable expectation of confidentiality.

Corpus source-specific finding

Anticipating litigation did not establish work-product protection on these facts.

Exact returned source excerpt

The court explained that the work product doctrine “provides qualified protection for materials **prepared by or at the behest of counsel** in anticipation of litigation or for trial.”  Even assuming that the AI-generated materials were prepared in anticipation of litigation, the court concluded that they were not prepared by or at the behest of an attorney and did not reflect defense counsel’s strategy.

Exact returned source excerpt

Judge Rakoff noted that the AI tool’s disclaimer that users have no expectation of confidentiality also undermined the work product doctrine claim.[[20]](#_ftn20) **Key Takeaways for Protecting Privilege and Broader Implications for Legal Research** The decision is the first we are aware of that finds that use of a consumer (non-enterprise) AI tool with otherwise potentially privileged materials may result in a loss of privilege.

Corpus source-specific finding

The commentators qualify the ruling and distinguish enterprise-tool recommendations from established holdings.

Exact returned source excerpt

The question presented – an issue of first impression – was: “whether when a user communicates with a publicly available AI platform in connection with a pending criminal investigation, are the communications protected by attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine?”  The court’s answer was no given the unique circumstances of the case – namely, that no lawyer was involved in the back-and-forth with the AI tool, and the tool itself was a public (*i.e.*, non-confidential) version.

Exact returned source excerpt

**The court’s decision is one of what will likely be many decisions analyzing the intersection of AI and legal privileges, but its holdings are confined to its facts, which are quite specific.

Corpus source-specific finding

Debevoise recommends documenting genuine counsel direction and the basis for protection.

Exact returned source excerpt

To bolster work product claims, clients and other non-lawyers using AI tools at the direction of counsel to assist with a legal case should make it clear in their prompts that they are acting at the direction of counsel.

Exact returned source excerpt

Courts have found that the work product doctrine can protect AI-generated content where the prompts and use of the tools fulfill the criteria to assert privilege.[[23]](#_ftn23) The government’s brief suggested that there could be instances where the work product doctrine protects content a client generates using AI, such as if “counsel directed the defendant to run the AI searches.”[[24]](#_ftn24) Finally, privilege logs should clearly denote the basis for the privilege and that the AI tool was used with the expectation of confidentiality.

Corpus source-specific finding

Covington recommends policies and training to reduce disclosure and discoverability risks.

Exact returned source excerpt

As courts continue to address these questions, there are concrete steps that in-house counsel and companies can take to minimize the risks posed by AI in the context of privilege, litigation, and government investigations: - Define Acceptable Use of AI – Implement a policy that defines the acceptable use of AI, including the use of AI in circumstances that may implicate legal considerations, such as the risk of creating discoverable documents.

Exact returned source excerpt

For example, consider limiting use to approved, proprietary enterprise AI tools, providing clear guidelines for the enterprise use of AI, implementing limits on the retention of AI prompts and outputs, and providing special guidance for uses that may implicate legal considerations.

Corpus framing of this source: United States v. Heppner: Public AI Use, Privilege and Work Product

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source explains Judge Jed S. Rakoff’s fact-specific ruling that a criminal defendant’s independently generated Claude materials were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine. It attributes the outcome to the absence of attorney direction and the use of a public AI service whose terms did not support confidentiality, while reporting the parties’ competing arguments and recommending controls for future AI-assisted legal work.

Corpus source-specific finding

The dispute arose from a defendant’s independent use of public generative AI to develop litigation materials after counsel had been retained.

Exact returned source excerpt

When Heppner was arrested, the FBI executed a search warrant and seized a number of documents and electronic devices, which included communications that Heppner had with a publicly available version of a generative AI platform that “prepared reports that outlined defense strategy, [and] outlined what he might argue with respect to the facts and the law” of what the government may charge.

Exact returned source excerpt

Heppner had engaged in these communications with the AI tool after he had received a grand jury subpoena and after it was clear from discussions with the government that he was the target of the investigation.

Corpus source-specific finding

The government and defense advanced competing characterisations of the documents under traditional privilege doctrines.

Exact returned source excerpt

The government moved for a ruling that the documents were not protected by privilege, arguing that the AI-generated documents were not confidential and not created for the purpose of obtaining legal advice.[[7]](#_ftn7) The government further contended that the defendant could not retroactively cloak unprivileged documents by later transmitting them to counsel.[[8]](#_ftn8) In addition, the government argued that the work product doctrine did not apply because the materials were prepared by the defendant on his own initiative and the doctrine “does not protect a layperson’s independent internet research.”[[9]](#_ftn9) During a pre-trial conference, Heppner’s counsel from Quinn Emanuel argued that the documents were protected by the attorney-client privilege because they incorporated information conveyed to their client during the course of the representation.[[10]](#_ftn10) With respect to the work product doctrine, Heppner’s counsel argued that a report created by a client in anticipation of litigation should be protected even if it was not done at the direction of legal counsel.[[11]](#_ftn11) **The Court’s Decision** Judge Rakoff issued an oral ruling that neither the attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine protected the AI-generated documents.[[12]](#_ftn12) The decision rests on traditional principles of privilege.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court held that attorney-client privilege did not attach because the exchange was not with counsel, was not confidential and was initiated independently rather than at counsel’s direction.

Exact returned source excerpt

Attorney-client privilege attaches to (1) communications **between a client and his or her attorney** (2) that are intended to be, and were, kept **confidential **(3) for the purpose of **obtaining or providing legal advice.**  The court held that at least two, if not all three, elements were not met.

Exact returned source excerpt

Not Communications with Counsel – The court held that the AI communications were not between Heppner and his counsel because the AI tool was not an attorney, and Heppner was consulting the tool entirely on his own (*i.e.*, not at the direction of counsel).

Corpus source-specific finding

The platform’s data practices supplied concrete support for the court’s confidentiality analysis.

Exact returned source excerpt

The court noted that the platform’s privacy policy specified that the platform collects data on user inputs and outputs, which it uses to train the tool, and reserved the right to disclose such data to third parties, including governmental regulatory authorities.

Exact returned source excerpt

In its motion, the DOJ noted that the applicable Anthropic Privacy Policy provides that prompts can be used to train their model and inputs may be disclosed to governmental regulatory authorities and third parties.[[21]](#_ftn21) Judge Rakoff ruled from the bench that the defendant “disclosed it to a third-party, in effect, AI, which had an express provision that what was submitted was not confidential.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court separately rejected work-product protection because neither counsel nor someone acting at counsel’s behest prepared the materials.

Exact returned source excerpt

The court explained that the work product doctrine “provides qualified protection for materials **prepared by or at the behest of counsel** in anticipation of litigation or for trial.”  Even assuming that the AI-generated materials were prepared in anticipation of litigation, the court concluded that they were not prepared by or at the behest of an attorney and did not reflect defense counsel’s strategy.

Exact returned source excerpt

[16]](#_ftn16) Second, Judge Rakoff held that the work product doctrine did not protect the documents.[[17]](#_ftn17) The work product doctrine protects (1) legal work product, (2) discussing legal strategy, (3) prepared by or at the direction of legal counsel, (4) in anticipation of litigation.[[18]](#_ftn18) Judge Rakoff rejected Heppner’s arguments that the work product doctrine could apply because the AI-generated reports did not reflect the legal strategy of Heppner’s legal counsel, although they contained theories generated by the client and Claude.[[19]](#_ftn19) Since neither Heppner nor the AI tool are legal counsel, and Heppner was not working at the direction of Heppner’s legal counsel, the materials were not protected by the work product doctrine.
