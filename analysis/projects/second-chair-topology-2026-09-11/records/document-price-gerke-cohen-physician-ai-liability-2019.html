Corpus framing of this source: Physician liability and the potential value of medical AI

Corpus synthesis of this source: In this 2019 JAMA Viewpoint, Price, Gerke and Cohen reason from general US tort principles to examine physicians’ potential liability when using medical AI. They argue that existing law can discourage clinicians from realising AI’s potential, while qualifying their analysis as a prediction in an area with essentially no relevant case law.

Corpus source-specific finding

Growing clinical adoption introduces risks from opaque and fallible recommendations.

Exact returned source excerpt

Glenn Cohen — JAMA 322(18), 1765–1766 (2019)* VIEWPOINT Artificial intelligence (AI) is quickly making inroads into medical practice, especially in forms that rely on machine learning, with a mix of hope and hype.<sup>1</sup> Multiple AI-based products have now been approved or cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and health systems and hospitals are increasingly deploying AI-based systems.<sup>2</sup> For example, medical AI can support clinical decisions, such as recommending drugs or dosages or interpreting radiological images.<sup>2</sup> One key difference from most traditional clinical decision support software is that some medical AI may communicate results or recommendations to the care team without being able to communicate the underlying reasons for those results.<sup>3</sup> Medical AI may be trained in inappropriate environments, using imperfect techniques, or on incomplete data.

Exact returned source excerpt

Even when algorithms are trained as well as possible, they may, for example, miss a tumor in a radiological image or suggest the incorrect dose for a drug or an inappropriate drug.

Corpus source-specific finding

The liability analysis extrapolates from malpractice principles rather than medical-AI precedents.

Exact returned source excerpt

### Medical AI and Liability for Physicians In general, to avoid medical malpractice liability, physicians must provide care at the level of a competent physician within the same specialty, taking into account available resources.<sup>4</sup> The situation becomes more complicated when an AI algorithmic recommendation becomes involved.

Exact returned source excerpt

In part because AI is so new to clinical practice, there is essentially no case law on liability involving medical AI.

Corpus framing of this source: Medical AI liability depends on standards of care and an evolving legal framework

Corpus synthesis of this source: Potential Liability for Physicians Using Artificial Intelligence uses illustrative clinical scenarios to distinguish patient harm, AI accuracy and probable legal liability. The article raises unresolved insurance questions, broadens responsibility beyond physicians and outlines possible legal changes rather than reporting empirical findings or settled outcomes.
