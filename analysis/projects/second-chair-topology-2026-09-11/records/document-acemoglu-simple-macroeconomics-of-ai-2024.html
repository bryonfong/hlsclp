SupportsAggregate productivity: Task speedups shrink during scaling

Open topic: Aggregate productivity: Task speedups shrink during scaling

Corpus framing of this source: The Simple Macroeconomics of AI: modest ten-year gains, uncertain distributional benefits

Corpus synthesis of this source: Daron Acemoglu’s May 2024 working paper uses a task-based model and existing empirical studies to estimate AI’s medium-term effects on US productivity, GDP, wages and welfare. He accepts that AI improves task-level productivity but argues that limited profitable adoption and harder-to-learn tasks constrain aggregate gains. He presents the calculations as suggestive, distinguishes GDP from welfare, and advocates creating new work that strengthens workers’ capabilities.

Corpus source-specific finding

A task-based framework links aggregate gains to affected tasks and their cost savings.

Exact returned source excerpt

I show that when AI’s microeconomic effects are driven by cost savings (equiva- lently, productivity improvements) at the task level—due to either automation or task complementarities—its macroeconomic consequences will be given by a version of Hulten’s theorem: GDP and aggregate productivity gains can be estimated by what fraction of tasks are impacted and average task-level cost savings.

Exact returned source excerpt

To gauge the extent of this impact, we can appeal to Hulten’s theorem, which provides a simple formula for competitive economies with constant returns to scale, specifying how micro- level productivity improvements translate into macro changes.

The baseline assumes AI economically affects 4.6% of tasks over ten years, not every exposed task.

Exact returned source excerpt

This procedure yields a wage bill-weighted share of exposed occupations equal to 20%.
