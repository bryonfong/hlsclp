Native topic findings

  1. The central dispute concerns how lawyers acquire independent judgment while learning to supervise AI—not simply whether schools should permit the technology.
  2. Berkeley illustrates a contested restrictive default, not a prohibition on all AI education or private study.
  3. Experimental productivity gains do not establish durable learning, professional competence or autonomous legal capability.
  4. The most direct reasoning experiment supports conditional effects rather than inevitable cognitive erosion or improvement.
  5. Student use and employer expectations motivate reform, but exposure, confidence and course availability remain imperfect measures of readiness.
  6. Assessment reform seeks observable understanding, but secure exams alone cannot establish practice readiness.
  7. Practical proposals make verification and independent defensibility explicit learning objectives.
  8. A stronger sceptical position challenges whether professional competence requires curricular AI adoption at all.
  9. AI may disrupt the work that finances and develops junior expertise, making training responsibility an institutional question.
  10. AI tutoring and simulation offer a possible replacement for lost practice opportunities, provided they elicit reasoning rather than supply answers.
  11. Implementation requires clear permissions, equitable access, institutional coordination and freedom to scrutinize tools.
