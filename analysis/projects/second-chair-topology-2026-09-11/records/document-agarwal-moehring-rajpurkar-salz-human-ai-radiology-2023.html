Corpus framing of this source: Radiology experiment: valuable human context does not guarantee effective AI assistance

Corpus synthesis of this source: Agarwal, Moehring, Rajpurkar and Salz’s NBER working paper (July 2023, revised November 2025) combines a randomized radiology experiment with belief-updating and economic delegation models. It finds unrealized gains from combining human and AI information, attributes those losses to systematic updating errors, and conditionally favors selective assignment over routine assistance. The authors explicitly leave generalization to generative AI and occupation-wide substitution unresolved.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors test collaboration rather than assume either replacement or augmentation is optimal.

Exact returned source excerpt

Yet, many disagree with Hinton’s proclamation that AI will replace radiologists.2 These skeptics argue that instead of human radiologists being replaced by AI, it is optimal for them to use AI assistance (Langlotz, 2019; Agrawal et al., 2019).

Exact returned source excerpt

We design and run an experiment with professional radiologists and develop an empiri- cal methodology that aims to answer these questions.3 Our experimental design compares human and AI performance, quantifies the predictive value of the information that humans hold but AI tools do not—henceforth termed contextual information—and tests whether AI assistance improves human performance.

Corpus source-specific finding

A 227-radiologist experiment separates the effects of AI predictions and clinical history.

Exact returned source excerpt

The experiment includes 227 professional radiologists recruited through teleradiology com- panies to diagnose retrospective patient cases.

Exact returned source excerpt

In the minimal information environment, we provide only the chest X-ray image to which we add either AI predictions, contextual information, or both.

AI outperforms most participants, but routine assistance produces no significant average diagnostic improvement.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus framing of this source: Combining Human Expertise with Artificial Intelligence: Experimental Evidence from Radiology

Corpus synthesis of this source: The paper frames radiology as a test case for deciding when superior AI predictions should automate decisions and when they should assist experts. Its substantive contribution is an incentivized experiment with high-skilled experts that measures behavior against a Bayesian benchmark, identifies systematic behavioral biases and connects those biases to the design of human–AI delegation. It also argues that economists can improve AI’s social value by studying human use and institutional design rather than technical development.

Corpus source-specific finding

The paper studies whether AI should replace or assist human decision-makers.

Exact returned source excerpt

This paper investigates the optimal form of collaboration between humans and AI for prediction problems.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors treat AI’s capacity for expert-level reasoning and performance as the premise for this delegation problem.

Exact returned source excerpt

But, in contrast to the innovations of the industrial revolutions, AI can perform tasks that require complex reasoning (Brynjolfsson and Mitchell, 2017).

Exact returned source excerpt

Indeed, a growing literature shows that AI can outperform humans in a host of tasks, including those typically performed by experts (Lai et al., 2021; Mullainathan and Obermeyer, 2019; Kleinberg et al., 2017; Agrawal et al., 2018).1 Radiology is an iconic example of this development.

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

Exact returned source excerpt

In addition to considerable legal and regulatory challenges that stand in the way of full automation, combining human expertise with AI input has potential gains that cannot be realized by exclusively relying on one or the other.
