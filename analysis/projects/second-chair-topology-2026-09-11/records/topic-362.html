Native topic findings

  1. The central question is how to develop independent legal judgment and AI competence together—not simply whether schools should allow AI.
  2. The sharpest disagreement concerns when AI exposure should begin and whether restrictive defaults protect or impede professional preparation.
  3. Experimental evidence shows task-dependent performance gains, but does not settle long-term effects on legal learning.
  4. Institutional practice combines expanding instruction with restrictions on assessed work, but adoption counts do not measure educational depth or effectiveness.
  5. Assessment reform seeks observable reasoning, but secure exams cannot replace every educational purpose of extended writing.
  6. Integration advocates define AI competence as practiced supervision, not tool familiarity or prompting alone.
  7. AI tutors and simulations offer a proposed way to expand deliberate practice as apprenticeship opportunities come under pressure.
  8. Equity supports both common access to AI training and limits on particular assessed uses.
  9. Implementation requires coordinated, revisable governance while preserving room for course-specific teaching.
