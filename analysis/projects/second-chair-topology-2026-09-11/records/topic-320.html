  1. The central proposition is organisational redesign: AI could turn legal expertise into repeatable services rather than merely accelerate individual lawyers.
  2. The strategic divide concerns who should deliver the work: software-enabled incumbents, integrated AI-native providers or stronger in-house teams.
  3. Corporate buyers shift the test from software adoption to the incremental value of outside counsel.
  4. Pricing may diversify without eliminating the billable hour, and efficiency does not guarantee lower prices or higher margins.
  5. Partnership incentives and implementation capacity can constrain transformation even when lawyers find AI useful.
  6. Firm-specific context, connected data and expert feedback underpin the proposed move from chat assistance to multi-step execution.
  7. Human oversight remains central, but its presence alone does not establish reliable legal outcomes.
  8. Observed uptake supports a case for practical usefulness, but does not establish firm-wide ROI or market transformation.
  9. The competitive outlook ranges from incumbent expansion to consolidation and AI-native entry, with important market-segment boundaries.
  10. Task displacement, project staffing and total employment are distinct claims, and speakers disagree about their relationship.
  11. Redesigning delivery also requires redesigning how lawyers acquire judgment, rather than assuming automation automatically improves training.
  12. The proposed human role extends beyond strategy to accountability, relationships and building legal operations; broader access remains a separate, unproven benefit.
