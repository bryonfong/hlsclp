Corpus framing of this source: AI could rebuild middle-class work by broadening access to expert judgment

Corpus synthesis of this source: David Autor’s February 2024 working paper argues that AI can expand the value and reach of human expertise rather than simply replace workers. He presents this as an attainable outcome, not a forecast, drawing on historical reasoning and studies of coding, writing, customer service and radiology. His central concern is the distribution and value of expertise, with direct implications for who can perform legal and other professional tasks.

Corpus framing of this source: Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs: Expertise, Augmentation and Institutional Choice

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source develops a historical and economic account of how AI may reshape the value of human expertise. It argues that AI can automate work but can also augment judgment, accelerate skill acquisition and create new expert roles. Experimental findings support near-term augmentation, while radiology illustrates that effective complementarity requires training. The source treats future labour outcomes as a scenario rather than a forecast and makes institutions and deployment incentives central to who benefits.

Corpus source-specific finding

Industrialization created mass expertise and helped build the middle class, whereas computerization displaced routine mass-expertise work and widened inequality.

Exact returned source excerpt

This virtuous combination of rising industrial productivity and rising demand for worker expertise helped build a new middle class in industrializing countries, one that could afford luxu- ries such as full wardrobes, factory-made household goods and new industrial products like electric toasters and irons.

Exact returned source excerpt

The unique power of the digital computer, relative to all technologies that preceded it, was its ability to cheaply, reliably and rapidly execute cognitive and manual tasks encoded in explicit, deterministic rules, i.e., what economists called “routine tasks” and what software engineers call programs (Autor et al., 2003).
