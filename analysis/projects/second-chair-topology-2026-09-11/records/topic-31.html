Native topic findings

  1. Legal procurement can benefit from new bidders without shifting work to them: competitive pressure on incumbents is the central mechanism.
  2. The empirical evidence shows lower prices, but entrant awards do not necessarily improve overall value.
  3. Apparent incumbency advantages may reflect buyer preferences for quality rather than favoritism.
  4. Nonbinding scoring weights may misrepresent how buyers actually trade quality against price.
  5. The practical recommendation is conditional auction design with continued supplier diversification.

An Empirical Analysis of Price, Quality, and Incumbency in Procurement Auctions

Academia & research · Foundations (pre-2015)

Corpus framing of this source: Open-ended legal procurement auctions: price savings, quality and apparent incumbency advantages

Corpus synthesis of this source: An Empirical Analysis of Price, Quality, and Incumbency in Procurement Auctions combines auction theory with GECF legal-services procurement data. The authors argue that discretionary award rules and new bidders can pressure high-quality incumbents to reduce prices, while quality preferences—not incumbency alone—can explain contract awards. They distinguish direct savings from indirect competitive benefits and qualify their recommendations by market conditions and concentration risks.
