Open topic: Court responsibility: AI requires lifecycle supervision

Corpus framing of this source: CEPEJ: rights-based, tested and user-controlled AI in judicial systems

Corpus synthesis of this source: The European Ethical Charter on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Systems and their Environment encourages judicial AI while making fundamental rights, human autonomy and public oversight conditions of adoption. These excerpts offer ethical recommendations and anticipated benefits, rather than measured performance findings; they explicitly attribute claims about greater legal predictability to tool developers.

Corpus source-specific finding

Encourage useful judicial AI, but make justice quality and fundamental rights—not efficiency alone—the governing objectives.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Charter is intended for public and private stakeholders responsible for the design and deployment of artificial intelligence tools and services that involve the processing of judicial decisions and data (machine learning or any other methods deriving from data science).

Exact returned source excerpt

It also concerns public decision-makers in charge of the legislative or regulatory framework, of the development, audit or use of such tools and services.

Corpus source-specific finding

Distinguish developers’ predictions from the Charter’s especially cautious approach to criminal justice.

Exact returned source excerpt

Judicial decision processing by artificial intelligence, according to their develop- ers, is likely, in civil, commercial and administrative matters, to help improve the predictability of the application of the law and consistency of court decisions, sub- ject to compliance with the principles set out below.

Exact returned source excerpt

In criminal matters, their use must be considered with the greatest reservations in order to prevent discrimina- tion based on sensitive data, in conformity with the guarantees of a fair trial.

Corpus source-specific finding

Preserve professional judgment and users’ ability to understand and challenge AI processing.

Exact returned source excerpt

■ User autonomy must be increased and not restricted through the use of artificial intelligence tools and services.

Exact returned source excerpt

■ Professionals in the justice system should, at any moment, be able to review judicial decisions and the data used to produce a result and continue not to be necessarily bound by it in the light of the specific features of that particular case.

Corpus source-specific finding

Research, inspect and evaluate tools before wider deployment, then assess their continuing impact.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is also essential to carry out research on the proposed applications and test them, both to understand their potential and their weaknesses and to be able to develop them further and adapt them to our needs.

Exact returned source excerpt

A right to examine the components and characteristics of the instruments proposed by the private sector (or those developed by independent and specialised public institutes, a solution which should be encouraged) seems equally important so that the justice service can effectively carry out its mission.

Corpus source-specific finding

Build policy through inclusive public debate and practical education rather than deployment alone.

Exact returned source excerpt

Lastly, the document suggests means of monitoring this phenome- non in the form of an ethical charter, emphasising the need for a cautious approach to the integration of these tools into public policies.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is essential that any public debate involves all the stakeholders, whether legal profes- sionals, legal tech companies or scientists, to enable them to convey the full scope and possible impact of the introduction of artificial intelligence applications in judicial systems and devise the ethical framework in which they must operate.

Corpus framing of this source: Predictive justice: statistical limits, bounded uses and user control

Corpus synthesis of this source: The European Ethical Charter on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Systems and their Environment distinguishes predictive software’s promises from demonstrated capability. It rejects reliable judicial prediction at the stage of development described, while identifying conditional uses for compensation scales and online dispute resolution and proposing ways for users to challenge statistical assessments.

Corpus synthesis of this source: The European Ethical Charter on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Systems and their Environment advocates cautiously tested AI assistance rather than unquestioned reliance on predictive justice. Corpus synthesis of this source: The European Ethical Charter on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Systems and their Environment describes expanding online dispute resolution (ODR), reports advocates’ access-to-justice expectations, and recommends assessing actual AI contributions, procedural integration and safeguards rather than assuming benefits.

Corpus source-specific finding

ODR is expanding beyond repetitive small claims into court proceedings and other dispute types.

Exact returned source excerpt

All European courts face to different degrees repetitive low-value civil litigation.

Exact returned source excerpt

Great Britain, the Netherlands and Latvia are examples of countries that have already implemented or are about to implement these types of more or less automated solution54s.

Corpus source-specific finding

Advocates expect automated dispute diagnosis to improve access to justice; the passage does not establish that outcome.

Exact returned source excerpt

For those who advocate such solutions, which are of interest to a num- ber of legal professions and the private sector, access to justice could be sig- nificantly improved by a broad solution combining ODR and AI (or at least expert systems, see section 3 above for the distinction).

Exact returned source excerpt

The idea is to take complainants through an automated diagnosis of the dispute by putting a number of questions, which are then processed by the machine, resulting in proposals for a solution.

Corpus framing of this source: CEPEJ: rights-based judicial AI, with strict limits on prediction and automated authority

Corpus synthesis of this source: The 2018 European Ethical Charter and its accompanying study encourage AI that improves judicial work while preserving fundamental rights and human control. They distinguish useful research and analytical assistance from unproven claims to predict judgments, explain judges’ motives or prescribe outcomes. The source combines ethical recommendations, legal safeguards, a qualified inventory of applications and discussion of experiments and disputed risk-assessment evidence.

Corpus source-specific finding

Judicial AI should improve justice under five governing principles, not pursue efficiency alone.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Charter is intended for public and private stakeholders responsible for the design and deployment of artificial intelligence tools and services that involve the processing of judicial decisions and data (machine learning or any other methods deriving from data science).

Exact returned source excerpt

The Charter defines five core principles to be respected in the field of Artificial Intelligence and justice: respect of fundamental rights; non-discrimination; quality and security; transparency, impartiality and fairness; “under user control”.

The study’s picture of European adoption is explicitly partial and time-bound.
