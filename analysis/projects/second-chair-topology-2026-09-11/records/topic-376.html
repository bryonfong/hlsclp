Native topic findings

  1. The central dispute concerns what benchmark success establishes: narrow legal capability, useful assistance or dependable autonomous lawyering.
  2. Measured improvements coexist with persistent variation across tasks, jurisdictions and model configurations.
  3. Direct human comparisons show genuine task-specific strengths without establishing general professional superiority.
  4. Complete-task grading reveals failures that high average or criterion-level scores can conceal.
  5. Citation reliability requires more than authentic case names or fluent explanations.
  6. Specialisation, retrieval and prompting can improve performance, but none provides a general reliability guarantee.
  7. Benchmark credibility depends on the evaluator as well as the evaluated model.
  8. Standalone output quality does not establish the benefit of giving humans AI assistance.
  9. Practical efficiency should include verification and correction, not merely generation speed or model cost.
  10. Sources disagree over the implications for replacement more than over the existence of useful capabilities.
  11. The shared operational implication is supervised, task-specific adoption backed by transparent testing.
