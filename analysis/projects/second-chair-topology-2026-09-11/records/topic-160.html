Native topic record

Service necessity: Verifiability cannot ensure proper treatment

Corpus synthesis: Contrasts restrictive honest-service theory with laboratory and field evidence showing that observability and lower prices need not reduce fraud.

This record retains the provider’s full available topic findings and source perspectives. It does not claim a new independent analytical review. Positions refer to this topic’s proposition: “Redirects” is not simple opposition, and “Unclear” is not support.

Declared lineage: Cross-domain institutional evidence: Innovation and market correction remain conditional on incentives, gatekeeping and measurement · Credence services: Verifiability alone cannot secure appropriate treatment

4 distinct stance-bearing works · Builds on: 2 · Supports: 2

Native topic findings

  1. The cluster frames expert services as a problem of judging necessity and suitability, not merely observing delivered work.
  2. The benchmark theory permits honest, efficient service, but only under specific institutional and pricing conditions.
  3. The laboratory evidence challenges the theoretical equivalence of liability and verifiability.
  4. Lower prices, greater trade and stronger reputation do not necessarily mean less fraud or higher efficiency.
  5. Field studies establish setting-specific misconduct, while behavioural evidence complicates purely financial explanations.
  6. More information and second opinions can improve discipline, but can also change incentives in adverse ways.
  7. Practical remedies must separately address diagnosis, truthful recommendations, adequate delivery and honest billing.

Corpus framing of this source: When prices and institutions can discipline credence-goods experts

Corpus synthesis of this source: Dulleck and Kerschbamer develop a unifying theoretical account of fraud in expert services. They distinguish inappropriate treatment from overcharging, identify conditions under which prices induce honest service, and explain how relaxing those conditions produces exclusion, mistreatment, repeated diagnosis or market breakdown. They support the motivation with previously published evidence and qualify the model with practical limits, unresolved incentives and research recommendations.

Corpus framing of this source: Fraud in expert-service markets: incentives, institutions and the limits of standard remedies

Corpus synthesis of this source: Balafoutas and Kerschbamer’s 2020 review synthesises theoretical, laboratory and field research on credence goods since Dulleck and Kerschbamer (2006). It explains how experts’ informational advantage enables mistreatment and fraudulent billing, and why liability, information, competition, reputation and second opinions produce context-dependent results. The authors advocate institution design that accounts for diagnostic costs and heterogeneous expert motivations rather than assuming that transparency or market pressure automatically ensures honest service.
