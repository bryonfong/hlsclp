Corpus framing of this source: Credence goods: expert fraud, conditional remedies and institutional design

Corpus synthesis of this source: Balafoutas and Kerschbamer’s 2020 review synthesises theory, laboratory experiments and field evidence on fraud in expert-service markets. They distinguish unsuitable provision from fraudulent billing and show why information, incentives and institutional safeguards must be considered together. Their contribution is a qualified synthesis of existing research, not a new empirical study or a universal prescription.

Corpus synthesis of this source: Balafoutas and Kerschbamer’s 2020 review synthesises theoretical models, laboratory experiments and field studies of expert-service markets, including lawyers among its examples. It distinguishes mistreatment from fraudulent billing and argues that remedies depend on consumer information, expert motivations, diagnostic effort and institutional design. The authors find liability comparatively robust, but qualify its practical feasibility and reject simple assumptions that competition, reputation, disclosure or second opinions invariably improve outcomes.

Corpus framing of this source: Fraud in expert-service markets: incentives, institutions and the limits of standard remedies

Corpus synthesis of this source: Balafoutas and Kerschbamer’s 2020 review synthesises theoretical, laboratory and field research on credence goods since Dulleck and Kerschbamer (2006). It explains how experts’ informational advantage enables mistreatment and fraudulent billing, and why liability, information, competition, reputation and second opinions produce context-dependent results. The authors advocate institution design that accounts for diagnostic costs and heterogeneous expert motivations rather than assuming that transparency or market pressure automatically ensures honest service.

Corpus synthesis of this source: The review integrates theoretical and empirical research on credence-goods markets. It documents unnecessary service provision and overcharging across sectors, analyses how third-party payment can alter seller and consumer behaviour, explains when liability and verifiability constrain fraud, and identifies digitalisation as both a source of new informational asymmetries and a potential consumer safeguard.
