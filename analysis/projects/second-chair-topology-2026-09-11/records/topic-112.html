Native topic findings

  1. The cluster frames automated-decision accountability as a combination of provider duties, consumer rights and public enforcement.
  2. The Colorado materials describe a change in regulatory design, not a single cumulative compliance regime.
  3. Notice and meaningful recourse recur across the frameworks, but human reconsideration and an alternative to automation are distinct rights.
  4. Colorado couples public enforcement with compliance accommodations while limiting some contractual transfers of discrimination liability.
  5. Version and status checks must precede reliance on the provisions or implementation dates.

Corpus framing of this source: Colorado S.B. 26-189: duties and consumer safeguards for consequential automated decisions

Corpus synthesis of this source: Colorado Senate Bill 26-189: Automated Decision-Making Technology sets out a replacement framework for Colorado’s 2024 AI consumer protections. Its substantive contribution is legislative: developer documentation, consumer disclosures and human-review rights, public enforcement, and limits on contractual liability shifting. The supplied material combines bill-summary language with amended legislative excerpts, rather than empirical findings or predictions.

Corpus source-specific finding

The bill replaces the 2024 framework with rules for technology that materially influences consequential decisions.

Exact returned source excerpt

In 2024, the general assembly enacted Senate Bill 24-205, which created consumer protections in interactions with artificial intelligence systems.

Exact returned source excerpt

The bill repeals and reenacts those provisions with new requirements regarding the use of automated decision-making technology SENATE in consequential decisions.

Corpus source-specific finding

Developers must equip deployers to use covered systems appropriately and maintain compliance records.

Exact returned source excerpt

The bill requires the developer of an ADMT (developer) that is used to materially influence a consequential decision (covered ADMT), starting January 1, 2027, to provide a deployer of a covered ADMT (deployer) with technical documentation describing the covered ADMT's intended uses, categories of training data, known limitations, and instructions for appropriate use and human review.

Exact returned source excerpt

Developers must notify deployers of material updates or modifications to the covered ADMT.

Corpus source-specific finding

Consumers receive notice, adverse-outcome explanations, data-correction rights and access to human reconsideration.

Exact returned source excerpt

The bill establishes consumer notice requirements, mandating that deployers provide clear and conspicuous notice to consumers at the point of interaction with a covered ADMT.

Exact returned source excerpt

A deployer is required to provide a consumer with a plain language description of a covered ADMT's role within 30 days after the covered ADMT makes a consequential decision that results in an adverse outcome for the consumer.

Report and Recommendations of the New York State Bar Association Task Force on Artificial Intelligence (April 2024)

Corpus framing of this source: Proposed New York protections for automated decision-making

Corpus synthesis of this source: The April 2024 NYSBA Task Force report describes a bill that would establish rights and safeguards for New York residents affected by automated decisions. The supplied excerpt reports legislative provisions; it does not establish the task force’s endorsement, present empirical findings or make predictions.

Corpus source-specific finding

The bill seeks lawful automated decisions with meaningful oversight and individual rights.

Exact returned source excerpt

The section on legislative intent says, in part: [T]he legislature declares that any New York resident affected by any system making decisions without human intervention be entitled to certain rights and protections to ensure that the system impacting their lives do so lawfully, properly, and with meaningful oversight.

Exact returned source excerpt

Among these rights and protections are (i) the right to safe and effective systems; (ii) protections against algorithmic discrimination; (iii) protections against abusive data practices; (iv) the right to have agency over one’s data; (v) the right to know when an automated system is being used; (vi) the right to understand how and why an automated system contributed to outcomes that impact one; (vii) the right to opt out of an automated system; and (viii) the right to work with a human in the place of an automated system.

Corpus source-specific finding

The bill defines algorithmic discrimination to include unjustified differential treatment or impact.

Exact returned source excerpt

“Algorithmic discrimination” means circumstances where an automated system contributes to an unjustified different treatment or impact which disfavors people based on their age, color, creed, disability, domestic violence victim status, gender identity or expression, familial status, marital status, military status, national origin, predisposing genetic characteristics, pregnancy-related condition, prior arrest or conviction record, race, sex, sexual orientation, or veteran status or any other classification protected by law.

Corpus source-specific finding

The bill would require safeguards before deployment and continuing monitoring afterward.

Exact returned source excerpt

Automated systems shall undergo pre-deployment testing, risk identification and mitigation, and shall also be subjected to ongoing monitoring that demonstrates they are safe and effective based on their intended use, mitigation of unsafe outcomes

Corpus synthesis of this source: The Colorado General Assembly’s enacted-bill summary describes reasonable-care duties, consumer safeguards, compliance defenses and attorney-general enforcement. The supplied source is marked superseded; it documents the 2024 enactment rather than establishing current law. It reports statutory provisions, not empirical findings, predictions or policy advocacy.

Corpus source-specific finding

The original enactment distinguished its effective date from the start of high-risk AI duties.

Exact returned source excerpt

APPROVED** by Governor May 17, 2024 **EFFECTIVE** May 17, 2024 *(Note: This summary applies to this bill as enacted.)* ## Prime Sponsors Senator Robert Rodriguez Representative

Exact returned source excerpt

Bill Summary: On and after February 1, 2026, the act requires a developer of a high-risk artificial intelligence system (high-risk system) to use reasonable care to protect consumers from any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination in the high-risk system.

Corpus source-specific finding

Developer compliance supports a rebuttable presumption of reasonable care.

Exact returned source excerpt

There is a rebuttable presumption that a developer used reasonable care if the developer complied with specified provisions in the act, including: - Making available to a deployer of the high-risk system a statement disclosing specified information about the high-risk system;Making available to a deployer of the high-risk system information and documentation necessary to complete an impact assessment of the high-risk system;Making a publicly available statement summarizing the types of high-risk systems that the developer has developed or intentionally and substantially modified and currently makes available to a deployer or other developer and how the developer manages any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination that may arise from the development or intentional and substantial modification of each of these high-risk systems; andDisclosing to the attorney general and known deployers or other developers of the high-risk system any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination, within 90 days after the discovery or receipt of a credible report from the deployer, that the high-risk system has caused or is reasonably likely to have caused.

Corpus source-specific finding

Deployer compliance combines risk governance with consumer notice and recourse.

Exact returned source excerpt

There is a rebuttable presumption that a deployer used reasonable care if the deployer complied with specified provisions in the act, including: - Implementing a risk management policy and program for the high-risk system;Completing an impact assessment of the high-risk system;Annually reviewing the deployment of each high-risk system deployed by the deployer to ensure that the high-risk system is not causing algorithmic discrimination;Notifying a consumer of specified items if the high-risk system makes, or will be a substantial factor in making, a consequential decision concerning the consumer;Providing a consumer with an opportunity to correct any incorrect personal data that a high-risk system processed in making a consequential decision;Providing a consumer with an opportunity to appeal, via human review if technically feasible, an adverse consequential decision concerning the consumer arising from the deployment of a high-risk system;Making a publicly available statement summarizing the types of high-risk systems that the deployer currently deploys, how the deployer manages any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination that may arise from deployment of each of these high-risk systems, and the nature, source, and extent of the information collected and used by the deployer; andDisclosing to the attorney general the discovery of algorithmic discrimination, within 90 days after the discovery, that the high-risk system has caused.

Exact returned source excerpt

A person doing business in this state, including a deployer or other developer, that deploys or makes available an artificial intelligence system that is intended to interact with consumers must ensure disclosure to each consumer who interacts with the artificial intelligence system that the consumer is interacting with an artificial intelligence system.
