Corpus framing of this source: Katyal’s Harvey promotion: advocacy assistance, backlash and lessons for law-firm communications

Corpus synthesis of this source: Rachel Williams’s LawFuel article reports the controversy over Neal Katyal’s attribution of a Supreme Court tariffs victory partly to Harvey. It distinguishes his account of AI-assisted preparation from critics’ objections to promotional overclaiming, and draws lessons about disclosure, tone and shared credit.

Corpus source-specific finding

Katyal presented Harvey as an aid to Supreme Court argument preparation.

Exact returned source excerpt

He then revealed the “twist”: behind the scenes, he had used Harvey, a custom AI tool trained on years of Supreme Court questions and opinions, to model likely questions and help shape answers for the argument.

Exact returned source excerpt

The tweet and related LinkedIn promotion positioned Harvey as a kind of super‑coach that anticipated the justices’ concerns, including an “escape route” that might bring Chief Justice John Roberts on board in the 6–3 ruling striking down the tariffs.

Corpus source-specific finding

Critics challenged the allocation of credit and promotional framing.

Exact returned source excerpt

Within hours, academics, appellate advocates and legal Twitter/X piled on, criticizing the post as self‑congratulatory, overstating the influence of one advocate and one AI tool on a major separation‑of‑powers case, and flirting with “influencer”‑style AI promotion.

Commentators also[ bristled at the suggestion that a proprietary AI “won” ](https://reason.com/volokh/2026/05/07/lets-talk-about-neal-katyals-ted-talk/)what many thought the justices were already inclined to do on the law, and at the TED‑style packaging of a serious public‑law case as a personal branding moment.

Katyal defended augmentation, not replacement, as his central message.

## Katyal’s Side of the Story When Lat pressed Katyal, the Milbank partner said his real goal was to start an honest conversation about how AI will reshape lawyering, not to claim robots had single‑handedly won a blockbuster case.

Exact returned source excerpt

The TED Talk itself, titled along the lines of AI’s ability to “predict” versus humans’ ability to “connect,” cast Harvey as “peripheral vision” for a litigation team rather than a replacement for lawyers.

Lat also raised an FTC‑style “material connection” question: did Katyal have any financial or promotional relationship with Harvey that should have been disclosed?

Exact returned source excerpt

Katyal responded that he has no equity stake, received no discounts and is simply a paying customer who worked with Harvey’s engineers for free, a claim Harvey’s spokesperson [echoed in comments to Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/katyals-boast-of-ai-role-in-tariff-win-draws-swift-blowback).

Williams recommends careful tone, disclosure and shared credit in AI marketing.

The episode shows how quickly a victory story can be reframed as cringe if it sounds like tech hype, minimises co‑counsel or treats a major public‑law case as a vehicle for personal and product branding.

For law‑firm marketers, the lesson is clear – AI war stories are powerful content, but the tone, disclosures and allocation of credit; ber they to clients, co‑counsel, funders and yes, the humans on the team matter just as much as the technology itself.
