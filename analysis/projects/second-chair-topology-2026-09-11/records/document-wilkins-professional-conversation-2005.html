Open topic: Career inequality: Professional change preserves unequal rewards

Corpus framing of this source: Wilkins on studying the restructuring of legal services

Corpus synthesis of this source: In this 2005 interview, David B. Wilkins sets out a research agenda for understanding global changes in legal and professional services and argues that law schools owe students and graduates systematic, unbiased information about those changes. He presents a research premise and institutional obligations, rather than empirical findings or specific forecasts.

Corpus source-specific finding

Wilkins frames global restructuring as a premise for research, not a demonstrated conclusion.

Exact returned source excerpt

What are the central questions the program wants to crack?** It starts from the premise that we are in the midst of a kind of global restructuring of legal and professional services, and we want to understand why it’s happened, how it’s happened, and what its implications are for the legal profession.

Corpus source-specific finding

The research should examine consequences beyond the legal profession.

Exact returned source excerpt

Here in the United States, law plays a fundamental role in the way in which we structure our lives, and lawyers are key players.

Exact returned source excerpt

To the extent that a fundamental shift is going on, what effect will it have on the profession and our wider political, economic and social lives?

Corpus source-specific finding

Law schools have a particular responsibility to study professional change and inform graduates.

Exact returned source excerpt

**Why is it the job of a law school, rather than a sociology department or a business school, to study that?** One could argue it’s everybody’s responsibility, but my argument would be, it’s particularly the responsibility of a law school, which claims to train people to be lawyers.

Exact returned source excerpt

It’s also the law school’s obligation to those lawyers after they graduate to provide them with unbiased and systematic information on the large questions that are affecting their professional careers and the world in which they live and compete.
SupportsLawyer development: AI renews lifelong competence reform

Open topic: Lawyer development: AI renews lifelong competence reform

Corpus framing of this source: Wilkins on law schools’ public obligations and institutional legal practice

Corpus synthesis of this source: Professor Wilkins argues that law schools should serve the public and prepare lawyers for increasingly institutional practice. He sees consolidation alongside diversification, rather than a settled trajectory toward uniformly larger firms.

Corpus source-specific finding

Law schools owe legal-service consumers an education that serves broader public purposes.

Exact returned source excerpt

I also think law schools have a professional obligation to the multiple consumers of legal services.

Exact returned source excerpt

After all, a license to practice law is something of a monopoly in the United States—at least, you can only become a lawyer if you go to law school.

Corpus source-specific finding

Legal education’s largest gap is its focus on individual decision-making rather than institutional practice.

Exact returned source excerpt

**Where do you see the biggest gap between what law schools teach and what lawyers do now?** The biggest gap stems from the fact that we focus almost entirely on law as an individual practice about individual decision making.

Exact returned source excerpt

But law is increasingly practiced in organizations and institutions of increasingly large and bureaucratic size.

Corpus source-specific finding

Consolidation does not establish that large merged firms will displace other practice models.

Exact returned source excerpt

Do you see one model winning over the other?** Well, I think we don’t know yet.

Exact returned source excerpt

A few years ago it was popular to talk about consolidation and to hypothesize that we would very quickly get to the point where law firms were the size of the major accounting firms.
