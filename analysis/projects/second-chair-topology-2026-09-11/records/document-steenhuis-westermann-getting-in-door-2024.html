Builds onWork redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Open topic: Work redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Corpus framing of this source: Getting in the Door: Streamlining Intake in Civil Legal Services with Large Language Models

Corpus synthesis of this source: The paper evaluates eight large language models as applicant-facing pre-screening tools for applying complex, jurisdiction-specific civil legal-aid intake rules. It finds promising but uneven performance, a conservative error pattern that generally avoids wrongful denials, and strong user-facing ratings despite missed follow-up questions. It recommends human oversight, model-specific prompt and rule refinement, bias evaluation, and deeper integration into legal-aid intake systems.

Corpus source-specific finding

Frames intake screening as an access-to-justice bottleneck that is unusually difficult to automate

Exact returned source excerpt

In the U.S., the first door—reaching a nonprofit legal aid lawyer—usually only opens after a poor person waits in a telephone intake queue for hours.

Exact returned source excerpt

The intake screening challenge is an important piece of the “access to justice” gap, which affects up to 92% of the poor worldwide.[28,52,14,34,47], Unresolved legal is- sues, like housing, custody, guardianship and domestic violence claims, can harm indi- viduals and burden society.

Corpus source-specific finding

Tests whether eight LLMs can apply jurisdiction-specific intake rules to layperson scenarios

Exact returned source excerpt

Discussion We investigated whether large language models can correctly determine eligibility of sce- narios under complex, jurisdiction-specific intake rules.

In E1, we compared the performance of 8 different LLMs on the task of deciding whether a scenario is eligible, not eligible, or more information is required to make this assess- ment.
