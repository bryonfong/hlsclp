Corpus synthesis of this source: Everyone’s a Law Company explains ALSP growth through the evolution of corporate legal buyers. The excerpt offers a historical account, referencing earlier reporting in The Practice, rather than quantitative evidence or a specific forecast or recommendation.

## How clients have evolved As we have <a href="https://thepractice.law.harvard.edu/article/in-house-counsel-movement/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">documented</a> elsewhere in *The Practice*, the in-house counsel movement that began in the United States in the late 1980s and early 1990s and then spread around the world fundamentally changed the corporate legal ecosystem by putting clients in the driver seat of the market for corporate legal services.

Exact returned source excerpt

Law firms, while still critical, were increasingly challenged by corporate legal departments who became both the chief diagnosticians of their companies’ legal problems and the chief purchasing agent responsible for solving them.

Corpus source-specific finding

Changes in client sourcing help explain ALSP growth.

Exact returned source excerpt

On one level, this shift had direct impacts on the traditional suppliers of outside legal services—law firms—including, for example, around the importance of specialization.

Exact returned source excerpt

On another level, however, the movement instigated a broader and more far-reaching overhaul of how companies sourced legal work, both internally and externally.

Corpus source-specific finding

Early-21st-century sourcing largely followed a two-option model: in-house work or outside law firms.

Exact returned source excerpt

At the beginning of the 21st century, in-house lawyers operated in a different environment from their present-day counterparts.

Exact returned source excerpt

Back then, the thinking was largely that they were hired as lawyers and should be able to handle most legal problems that came their way.

Open topic: Provider convergence: Buyers coordinate an unbundled market

Corpus framing of this source: Everyone’s a Law Company: matching legal work to a broader provider market

Corpus synthesis of this source: The Practice editorial team’s 2019 article describes ALSPs as established participants in a changing legal-services market. Through practitioner accounts and organizational examples, it links their growth to more deliberate client sourcing, explains law firms’ continuing advantages and adaptations, and notes changing career perceptions and the difficulty of navigating increased choice.

Corpus source-specific finding

ALSPs are established providers, not merely new alternatives to law firms.

Exact returned source excerpt

Wilkins and Maria José Esteban Ferrer detail in this issue’s <a href="https://thepractice.law.harvard.edu/article/taking-the-alternative-out-of-alternative-legal-service-providers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">lead article</a>, the term “alternative legal service provider” (ALSP) can be misleading.

Exact returned source excerpt

Not only are these so-called “alternatives” increasingly used across the legal ecosystem by clients of all sizes and types, but many of today’s major ALSP players have in fact been around for decades.

Client process redesign and legal operations have helped ALSPs grow.

Exact returned source excerpt

> While the journey largely began with e-discovery, clients eventually turned to reexamine other aspects of their legal work.

Exact returned source excerpt

Exact returned source excerpt

For clients, the key dimension is quality—both quality of the process being applied and quality of each piece within it—and the culture change described above has enabled them to seek quality from a variety of new sources.

With our bottom-up thinking about evaluating activities, it’s really about which company aligns to the activity and outcome we’re trying to achieve.
