Corpus framing of this source: Upsolve’s First Amendment challenge to New York’s UPL restrictions

Corpus synthesis of this source: The petition argues that New York’s prohibition on unlicensed legal advice regulates speech by subject matter and therefore triggers strict scrutiny. It contends that the Second Circuit misapplied Supreme Court precedent, widened an inter-circuit conflict, and treated occupational licensing as if it received special First Amendment treatment. It recommends holding the petition for Chiles v. Salazar or granting review if Chiles does not resolve the issue.

Corpus source-specific finding

The petition argues that the Second Circuit wrongly classified New York’s restriction as content-neutral merely because it uses the purpose of conveying legal advice as its trigger.

Exact returned source excerpt

As a direct result, the panel ruled it is content-neutral for New York to prohibit speech that has the “purpose” of conveying particular content: “rendering [ ] legal advice or opinions[.]” App.

Exact returned source excerpt

Yes, New York’s rules regulate speech by reference to its purpose—the purpose of “rendering [ ] legal advice or opinions”—but that purpose is simply a proxy.

Corpus source-specific finding

The petition grounds its argument in Reed and City of Austin, which it reads as preventing governments from disguising subject-matter distinctions as function-or-purpose rules.

Exact returned source excerpt

Some content-based distinctions are “obvious, defining regulated speech by particular subject matter, and others are more sub- tle, defining regulated speech by its function or pur- pose.” Ibid.

Exact returned source excerpt

In City of Austin, this Court clarified that laws that require a regulator to consider the function or purpose of speech are not “always content based” but reaffirmed that “a regulation of speech cannot es- cape classification as facially content-based simply by swapping an obvious subject-matter distinction for a ‘function or purpose’ proxy that achieves the same re- sult.” 596 U.S.

The petition claims a split of authority over viewpoint-neutral function-or-purpose restrictions.

The petition argues that ordinary First Amendment scrutiny applies to occupational-licensing laws, including UPL restrictions.

Broadly, both Chiles and the Second Circuit’s rule concern how this Court’s ordinary First Amend- ment principles interact with state occupational-li- censing powers.

The obvious answer would be that li- censing laws are subject to First Amendment scrutiny just like any other exercise of state power—and that is precisely what this Court held just a few years ago in rejecting the so-called “professional speech doc- trine.” Nat’l Inst.
