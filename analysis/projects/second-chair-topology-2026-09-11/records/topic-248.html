Legal automation: Classification success cannot prove reasoning

Corpus synthesis: Separates documented labour savings from litigation prediction and general competence, emphasizing data, expertise and task design.

This record retains the provider’s full available topic findings and source perspectives. It does not claim a new independent analytical review. Positions refer to this topic’s proposition: “Redirects” is not simple opposition, and “Unclear” is not support.

Native topic findings

  1. The central divide concerns task automation versus legal reasoning, not whether AI can perform any legal work.
  2. Document classification supplies concrete evidence of labour savings, but its results do not establish general legal competence.
  3. Litigation prediction attracts optimism about better advice, alongside skepticism about autonomous reasoning.
  4. LLMs change the technical basis of the debate, but neither conversational fluency nor older automation proves their legal reliability.
  5. Data preparation, domain expertise and task design remain practical determinants of automation.
  6. Efficiency can change staffing and learning opportunities without establishing wholesale displacement or inevitable deskilling.
  7. Lower processing costs do not by themselves resolve access-to-justice or deployment constraints.
