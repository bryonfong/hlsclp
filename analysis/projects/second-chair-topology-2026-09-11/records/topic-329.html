Native topic findings

  1. The central dispute is whether AI breaks hourly billing’s economics or changes how firms earn within them.
  2. Fewer hours per matter do not automatically mean lower profits: rates, collections, costs and demand also matter.
  3. Observed pricing practices show coexistence, while strong financial results permit competing interpretations.
  4. Hourly billing persists partly because legal scope, quality and value are difficult to specify—not simply because firms resist change.
  5. Corporate buyers can force efficiency sharing, but their power, information and willingness to change remain uneven.
  6. Pricing reform implicates partnership governance, investment and staffing—not just invoice design.
  7. A practical middle position matches pricing and delivery to the task, while preserving explicit scope and quality controls.
  8. Who captures AI savings is a separate ethical and commercial question from whether firms retain hourly billing.
  9. Commercial adaptation has consequences for development, inclusion and workload, but benefits do not follow automatically from automation.
  10. Elite-firm profitability cannot stand in for the economics of the whole profession.
