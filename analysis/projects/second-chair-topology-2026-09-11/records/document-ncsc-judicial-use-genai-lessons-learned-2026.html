Corpus synthesis of this source: The March 2026 TRI/NCSC report synthesises interviews with early-adopter US judges to inform court policy and training. Corpus source-specific finding

The report offers qualitative evidence from early adopters, not a judiciary-wide adoption estimate.

Exact returned source excerpt

Background In October and November of 2025, 13 interviews of approximately 60 minutes in duration were conducted with state and federal judges serving in 10 different states.

Exact returned source excerpt

Interviewees reflected a cross-section of the judiciary in the USA, with participation from justices (including chiefs) of courts of last resort, judges of intermediate appellate courts, and trial judges.

Corpus source-specific finding

Interviewees unanimously reserved judicial decisions for humans.

Exact returned source excerpt

GenAI can support, but not supplant, the essential work of judges as human decision makers Every judge who participated in the interviews was using GenAI in their own individual way.

Exact returned source excerpt

But there was unanimous consensus among the judges that, regardless of how they are using GenAI, judges must always remain “the deciders” who determine the ultimate outcome of any legal decision before them.

Corpus source-specific finding

Judges reported efficiency benefits from bounded assistance, while distinguishing existing uses from possibilities.

Exact returned source excerpt

As is discussed further below, the top benefit the judges identified in their use of GenAI was increased efficiency, and their use of GenAI to help streamline certain tasks is saving them time.

Exact returned source excerpt

When GenAI helps a judge brainstorm ideas for an upcoming speech or prepare a table of speakers in a lengthy transcript, this frees the judge up to focus their attention on applying the law to the facts and ensuring a just outcome.

Corpus source-specific finding
