Corpus framing of this source: AI as a proposed route beyond scarcity in civil legal help

Corpus synthesis of this source: Margaret Hagan proposes a technology-centered theory of change for access to justice. Drawing on interviewees’ assumptions about scarce human capacity, she argues that AI could expand legal help by changing its production, while complementing institutional reform. The supplied passages offer conditional predictions and workflow proposals rather than measured results from deployments.

Corpus source-specific finding

Hagan identifies labor scarcity as an assumption running through the interviews and existing reform theories.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Scale Problem Revisited A thread running through the interviews was the assumption that access to justice ser- vices are inherently labor-intensive and therefore inherently scarce.

Exact returned source excerpt

Every theory of change discussed in this Article operates within this scarcity constraint: the question is how to allocate limited resources more effectively, create pressure for more resources, or change institutional culture to use existing resources better.

What a Technology-Centered Theory of Change Would Look Like A tenth theory of change might be stated as follows: If we build AI-powered legal help tools that are accurate, accessible, and trustworthy, and embed them in the pathways where people already encounter legal problems, then we can dramatically expand the number of people who receive meaningful help—shifting the field from scarcity-driven triage to abundance-oriented service design.

In practice, this might involve AI-assisted self-help tools embedded in court websites that guide litigants through filing, responding, and preparing for hearings; intelligent triage systems that connect people to the right level of help (full representation, limited-scope assistance, self-help tools, or non-legal services) based on the complexity and stakes of their situation; AI-augmented legal aid workflows where attorneys handle the judgment- intensive work while AI handles drafting, research, and routine communications; and community-based “navigator” models where non-lawyers use AI tools to provide substantive legal guidance that would previously have required an attorney.

Corpus framing of this source: Paths Toward Access to Justice at Scale: Evaluating Theories of Change in the Civil Justice System

Corpus synthesis of this source: Margaret Hagan argues that access-to-justice initiatives often fail to progress from pilots to durable systemic reform because the field leaves its causal assumptions implicit. Drawing on eleven expert interviews and relevant systems-change literature, she develops a Knowledge–Pressure–People framework, assesses nine practitioner theories of change, and proposes AI-enabled legal help as a tenth, potentially scale-changing mechanism that still requires institutional reform, quality controls and equity safeguards.
