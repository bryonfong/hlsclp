Corpus framing of this source: The Algorithm as a Human Artifact: Implications for Legal [Re]Search

Corpus synthesis of this source: The article combines a comparative empirical study of six legal research databases with an argument that search algorithms embody human choices, classifications and assumptions. It finds substantial variation in top-ranked cases, explores possible reasons for older providers’ higher measured relevance, identifies methodological limits, and recommends redundant search strategies, researcher education and greater algorithmic accountability.

Corpus source-specific finding

Legal search algorithms embody human judgments rather than operating as neutral or interchangeable tools.

Most people do not think about the underlying structures of the technologies they use.39 But some inquiry into the forces at work in the legal research environment, at this moment when so much of the work is truly invisible, is certainly called for.40 It is time to examine technical bias in legal computer systems.41 Technical bias is built into systems.

Corpus source-specific finding

The comparative study found little overlap among databases searching substantially similar case corpora.

Exact returned source excerpt

This study used Casetext, Fastcase, Google Scholar, Lexis Advance, Ravel, and Westlaw to look at the differences in results when six different sets of engineers set out to solve the same problem.

By limiting searches to the subset of reported cases within a specific jurisdiction and using the same search terms in each database, it is possible to compare search results in a nearly identical group of documents.112 If, for example, a search is executed in a database of all reported cases from the Northern District of California, there should be, in theory, an iden- tical set of documents regardless of which legal database is searched.
