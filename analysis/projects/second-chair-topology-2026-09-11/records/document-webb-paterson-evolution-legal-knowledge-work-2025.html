Corpus framing of this source: GenAI as a digital law clerk: limited substitution, potentially deeper professional change

Corpus synthesis of this source: Julian Webb and Jeannie Paterson’s April 2025 draft combines a four-archetype framework with Australian adoption reports and overseas studies of legal competence and working time. They argue that current genAI chiefly supports supervised, lower-complexity tasks, while its longer-term significance may lie in redistributing legal knowledge and changing firms’ business models and professional identity. They qualify their conclusions because adoption evidence is commercially mediated, much functional evidence predates genAI, and independent performance testing remains limited.

Corpus source-specific finding

Current genAI fits the digital law clerk better than the autonomous lawyer.

Exact returned source excerpt

On the basis of this analysis, we argue that, by virtue of a mix of cultural, economic, and technical constraints, genAI is both unevenly distributed across the legal ecosystem, and currently over-hyped, operating chiefly as a kind of ‘digital law clerk’, substituting for and augmenting human work on only a limited range of lower level diagnostic tasks.

Exact returned source excerpt

Here we see AI technologies primarily as a supplement to everyday practice, operating on a range of lower complexity (but often time-intensive) tasks that, typically, might be conducted by an able law clerk or graduate under supervision, or by a less-experienced associate solicitor.

Corpus source-specific finding

Four descriptive archetypes distinguish degrees of integration without predicting an inevitable progression.

Exact returned source excerpt

These, we submit, can assist us not just to examine the current gap between hype and reality, but to taxonomise future socio-technical shifts in AI’s influence on legal practice, if, as some predict, genAI is a step on the road to genuinely ‘agentic’ AI, whether as enacted through combinations of genAI and ML tools able to perform discrete tasks with a higher degree of autonomy than current tools, or some yet to be developed technology.13 We call these archetypes the robot lawyer; the lawyer as cyborg, the lawyer as inforg, and the digital law clerk.
