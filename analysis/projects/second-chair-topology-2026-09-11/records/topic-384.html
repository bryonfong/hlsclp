  2. Experimental and workplace evidence supports substantial, but outcome-specific, productivity gains.
  3. The claim that AI benefits weaker workers most has important counterexamples.
  4. Legal evidence favours distinguishing speed, quality and productive use rather than assuming access is sufficient.
  5. Better assisted output does not necessarily mean that workers learn more.
  6. Freelance markets provide evidence of contracting demand and changing competitive rewards.

  9. Exposure and usage evidence map potential transformation, not realized replacement.
  10. AI can change task allocation and collaboration before changing headcount.
  11. Aggregate productivity and distributional forecasts turn on assumptions beyond task-level gains.
  12. The longer-run practical issue is preserving expertise formation while measuring who gains and who loses.
