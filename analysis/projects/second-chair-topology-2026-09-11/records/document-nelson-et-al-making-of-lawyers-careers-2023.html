Open topic: Career inequality: Professional change preserves unequal rewards

Corpus framing of this source: Persistent inequality and varied routes to success in lawyers’ careers

Corpus synthesis of this source: The Making of Lawyers' Careers combines longitudinal surveys, interviews and career tracking to examine opportunity and inequality in the US legal profession. The supplied catalogue and David Wilkins interview emphasise durable demographic and educational advantages, institutional barriers to diversity, and satisfying careers outside elite firms. Wilkins cautions that disruption need not dismantle professional hierarchies.

Corpus source-specific finding

The study follows a broad cohort over roughly two decades rather than relying on a snapshot of legal careers.

Exact returned source excerpt

This and many other myth-busting conclusions are the result of “After the JD,” a quantitative and qualitative analysis that includes more than 5,000 law graduates who entered the bar in 2000.

Exact returned source excerpt

Starting in 2000, the authors collected over 10,000 survey responses from more than 5,000 lawyers, following these lawyers through the first twenty years of their careers.

Corpus source-specific finding

Careers reproduce wider social inequalities, although individuals retain agency.

Exact returned source excerpt

Their findings show that lawyers’ careers both reflect and reproduce inequalities within society writ large.

Exact returned source excerpt

They also reveal how individuals exercise agency despite these constraints.

Corpus source-specific finding

Elite law-school advantages increase rather than disappear as careers develop.

Exact returned source excerpt

But on the contrary, the benefits of going to a top-ranked law school actually increase over the course of a lawyer’s career in terms of income, opportunities for prestigious jobs, and the ability to repay law school debt, according to the findings of a 20-year longitudinal study of the careers of lawyers.

Exact returned source excerpt

David Wilkins, part of the core research team that launched a longitudinal study on lawyers’ lives,  describes what’s changed and — despite best intentions — what hasn’t in the past 20 years Sep 30, 2024 By [Elaine McArdle](https://hls.harvard.edu/authors/elaine_mcardle/) People often assume that the advantages of attending an elite law school evaporate over the course of a lawyer’s career, because the quality of the work lawyers produce becomes more important than academic credentials.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors seek practical and public uses for the research; accompanying commentary adds distinct recommendations.

Exact returned source excerpt

Wilkins says the authors hope the abundance of “rich data” the book offers will be useful to three groups in particular: law students as they consider what they want from their careers; law schools as they design curricula to best serve students; and the public, because, he says, “What the profession looks like, who has access to it, and what the structures of opportunity are, are matters of interest and importance and concern far beyond the legal profession to our society as a whole.” “I’m glad I did it, but if I’d had any idea how long it would take me, I’m not sure I would have entered into this project,” Wilkins adds, with a chuckle.

Exact returned source excerpt

*The Making of Lawyers’ Careers* should be a required reading in these types of classes.

Corpus framing of this source: Legal conservatism can preserve public values while obstructing professional change

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source argues that lawyers’ habit of questioning change can both protect foundational institutions and impede reform within their profession. It acknowledges lawyers’ mixed societal role and calls for research on how these tensions shape careers, rather than offering empirical findings or a specific forecast.

Open topic: Work redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Corpus framing of this source: The Making of Lawyers' Careers: Inequality and Opportunity in the American Legal Profession

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source argues that major changes in the profession have not displaced durable inequalities in career outcomes. It attributes persistent racial disparities primarily to hiring, development, promotion and cultural structures that reproduce an image of the “right” lawyer, while acknowledging continuing racism. It also documents fulfilling careers among solo and small-firm lawyers and cautions that future disruptions may be absorbed by existing professional hierarchies.
