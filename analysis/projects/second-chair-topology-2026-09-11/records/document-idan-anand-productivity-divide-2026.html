Corpus framing of this source: AI interaction competence shapes uneven productivity gains

Corpus synthesis of this source: Anand and Idan’s working paper reports a randomized experiment simulating early-career knowledge work. It attributes uneven gains from GenAI to users’ ability to elicit, filter and verify outputs, and argues that training and structured workflows can reduce performance disparities. The supplied excerpts report directional findings rather than effect sizes and extend task-performance results to managerial implications.

Corpus source-specific finding

GenAI improved average task performance, but gains varied substantially across participants.

Exact returned source excerpt

We report results from a randomized controlled experiment in which participants—analogs of early-career knowledge work- ers—were assigned to self-study a technical domain using either traditional resources or large-language- model (LLM) assistance.

Exact returned source excerpt

On average, GenAI access significantly increased task performance, but the dis- tribution of gains was highly uneven.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI Interaction Competence, not GPA or prior knowledge, predicted improvements.

Exact returned source excerpt

Improvements were not predicted by GPA or prior knowledge, but by AI Interaction Competence (AIC) — the ability to elicit, filter, and verify model outputs.

Exact returned source excerpt

High-AIC participants realized outsized gains; low-AIC participants saw limited or even negative marginal returns.

Corpus source-specific finding

Process scaffolding can narrow performance disparities without sacrificing average performance.

Exact returned source excerpt

A scaffolding intervention (conceptual maps) reduced outcome variance, indicating that standardized workflows can mitigate inequality in AI-mediated performance.

Exact returned source excerpt

Our study complements this literature by providing controlled, causal micro-evidence: it isolates how differences in AIC levels shape productivity outcomes and demonstrates that lightweight process interventions—conceptual scaffolding and training—reduce variance without lowering mean performance.
