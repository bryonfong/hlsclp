Corpus framing of this source: Hansmann on ownership, asset partitioning and organizational form

Corpus synthesis of this source: Hansmann presents an economic framework in which firms combine common control with legally partitioned bonding assets, while ownership assigns control and residual returns to particular patrons. He explains organizational forms as tradeoffs among contracting, monitoring, opportunism, risk, managerial agency and collective decision-making costs. The supplied excerpts apply that framework to corporations, partnerships, nonprofits, professional firms, statutory governance forms and governments, and cautiously predict more fragmented assets and heterogeneous ownership arrangements.

The Boundaries of the Firm: Control versus Assets We observed earlier that the “boundary of the firm” might be defined in terms of the scope of control -- that is, how extensive is the set of contractual and ownership rights that are subject to common control.

Exact returned source excerpt

But, as a legal entity, a firm is also defined by a set of bonding assets.

Corpus source-specific finding

Entity shielding is more fundamental than limited liability

Exact returned source excerpt

Yet entity shielding -- while until recently largely ignored (and even lacking a name) in the Anglo-American literature in both law and economics -- is the more elemental rule (Hansmann & Kraakman 1999, 2000; Hansmann, Kraakman, & Squire 2006).

Exact returned source excerpt

All standard legal forms for enterprise organization today offer entity shielding – that is, they give the creditors of the organization first claim on the organization’s assets, ahead of the individual creditors of the firm’s owners.

Corpus source-specific finding

Asset partitioning lowers monitoring costs and supports transferable ownership, but can enable opportunism

Exact returned source excerpt

In the conventional business corporation with multiple shareholders, for example, limited liability shields the personal (and other business) assets of the firm’s owners from the claims of the firm’s creditors, while entity shielding protects the firm’s assets from the claims of the shareholders’ personal creditors.

Exact returned source excerpt

In firms with multiple owners, moreover, both these forms of asset partitioning not only offer the monitoring economies described above, but facilitate the transferability of shares by making the creditworthiness of the firm largely independent of the personal creditworthiness of the firm’s owners.

Indeed, security interests and legal entities seem likely to become indistinguishable at the margins as the two devices continue to evolve.23 The tradeoff here is between reducing monitoring costs and raising notice costs, both of which generally result from more articulated asset partitioning.

Exact returned source excerpt

That tradeoff has gradually shifted in favor of more refined partitioning as information costs of giving notice have decreased.

Corpus source-specific finding

Professional-firm ownership restrictions can protect incumbents and inefficient practices

Exact returned source excerpt

If risk capital for, say, law firms can only be obtained from the lawyers in the firms, then the prosperous older partners of established firms, who have the most capital to supply, can get an above-market rate of return on their capital; they need not compete with new investor-financed entrants.

Exact returned source excerpt

Likewise, traditional modes of practice, familiar to senior professionals but perhaps no longer efficient, will not be under strong pressure from new firms.

Corpus source-specific finding

Nonprofit forms address contracting failures but may persist for reasons unrelated to present efficiency

Exact returned source excerpt

The best explanation is that, in the absence of competing “high-powered” profit incentives, the managers’ actions are guided by “low-powered” nonpecuniary incentives, including pride, professionalism, and identification with the goals of the organization (Glaeser & Shleifer, 2001).

Exact returned source excerpt

While the nonprofit form seems most commonly to be a response to the problems of contracting when quantity is unobservable, there are also situations in which they are evidently a response to unobservable or unverifiable quality.16 Nursing homes for the elderly – where, for example, sedating patients into inexpensive tractability is a tempting strategy for the producers -- are evidently an example.
