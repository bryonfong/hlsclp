Corpus synthesis of this source: In Regulatory Markets: The Future of AI Governance, Hadfield and Clark compare European, American, British and Chinese AI governance. They argue that different legal approaches still struggle to translate broad risk principles into operational requirements, question reliance on industry standard-setting, and propose regulatory markets as a potential remedy. Corpus framing of this source: Regulatory markets: public outcomes, competing private regulators

Corpus synthesis of this source: In Regulatory Markets: The Future of AI Governance, Gillian K. Hadfield and Jack Clark propose government-licensed markets for AI regulatory services. They combine a critique of existing governance with institutional design, hypothetical applications and predictions about investment and international coordination. They recommend selective deployment, while stressing that competition cannot replace political will, technical expertise or well-funded public oversight.

Corpus framing of this source: Regulatory markets for technically capable, democratically accountable AI governance

Corpus synthesis of this source: Hadfield and Clark argue that current AI-governance regimes cannot keep pace with complex, rapidly evolving systems because conventional law lacks operational detail while private standard-setting lacks public accountability. After comparing the European Union, United States, United Kingdom and China, they propose competitive markets of licensed private regulators: governments define outcomes, regulated firms buy approved regulatory services, private regulators develop technical controls, and governments audit their results. They present this as an urgent but conditional institutional innovation, not a universal solution.
