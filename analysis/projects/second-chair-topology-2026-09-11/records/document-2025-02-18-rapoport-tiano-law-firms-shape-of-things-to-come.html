Law firms still tend to bill by the hour, and law firms are still shaped in a way that reflects what Marc Galanter and Thomas Palay have termed the ‘[tournament of lawyers](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/T/bo3642788.html)’.

Open topic: Law-firm pyramid: Efficiency threatens revenue and training

Corpus framing of this source: GenAI could dismantle junior-hour economics and force firms to demonstrate added value

Corpus synthesis of this source: In “Law Firms: The Shape of Things to Come” (2025), Nancy Rapoport and Joseph Tiano argue that GenAI will undermine the staffing and revenue logic of traditional law firms. They predict fewer junior associates, client-driven workflow changes and declining reliance on hourly compensation, while allowing that premium hourly rates could survive for sophisticated work. Their contribution develops an economic argument and forecasts rather than presenting measured outcomes.

Corpus source-specific finding

The pyramid rewards associate hours and partner income rather than necessarily rewarding client value.

Exact returned source excerpt

Often referred to as the ‘pyramid’ model, this common law firm shape is a highly leveraged structure where throngs of lower-level legal professionals have historically handled an enormous volume of routine, low-value tasks at handsome hourly rates, generating high-value invoices that benefit mostly a small group of equity partners sitting at the pyramid’s apex.

Exact returned source excerpt

Once junior legal professionals master the scutwork, they get higher-value work, but they also start competing against each other to see which ones will stay at the firm and eventually get to the top of the pyramid.

Corpus source-specific finding

Entrenched incentives and weak alternatives have preserved hourly billing despite acknowledged shortcomings.

Exact returned source excerpt

For decades, we’ve known that the billable-hour economic model is fraught with inefficiency and poor scalability, talent retention challenges, disincentives to innovate, and ethical concerns around reasonable billing, but the needle hasn’t moved much, if at all, partly because of the unpredictability of work volume when handled by humans.

Exact returned source excerpt

Despite their recognition of the shortcomings of the billable hour model, law firms have not changed the model because, after all, it is making them wealthy.

Corpus source-specific finding

GenAI will, the authors predict, weaken junior-hour revenues and displace hourly billing as the predominant compensation model.

Exact returned source excerpt

But we think that GenAI will change the legacy economic model because we are beginning to see widespread adoption of GenAI as a legal service delivery tool.

Exact returned source excerpt

When routine work moves from humans to computers, the bottom tier of the pyramid will no longer generate the rich results that law firm partners want.

Corpus source-specific finding

Powerful clients could force automation and bring drafting work in-house.

Exact returned source excerpt

We predict that clients with leverage will insist that law firms use GenAI, not junior legal professionals, on routine low-value tasks and that large clients with the right resources will start to pull certain types of work back in-house, perhaps by having a combination of inside lawyers and GenAI develop first drafts, and then handing those drafts to a law firm to ask, ‘how can you improve on this draft?’ Either way, workflow process changes of this nature will eventually drive law firms to change how they deliver legal services and how they account for the value that they deliver to clients.

Exact returned source excerpt

Therefore, law firms must figure out how to bill for GenAI-enabled tasks while finding ways to describe their own value-added services.

Corpus source-specific finding

Firms should anticipate client changes, separate commoditised work from bespoke advice and consider premium pricing for sophisticated work.

Exact returned source excerpt

The winning law firms in this new type of tournament will be the ones that anticipate this change in client behavior.

Exact returned source excerpt

Some are already shifting their own mix of work, commoditizing parts of it, such as first drafts of routine documents and “first pass” document review and leaving other parts ‘bespoke.’ The beauty of commoditized work, in additional to being predicably income-generating, is that it frees up lawyer time to think about more novel questions and perform more complex work.
