Open topic: Big Four: Integration matters more than replacement

Corpus framing of this source: The Big Four’s return to law through integrated global business services

Corpus synthesis of this source: In this 2016 article, David B. Wilkins and María J. Esteban Ferrer argue that the Big Four rebuilt and expanded their legal networks after post-Enron regulation appeared to end their ambitions. Their website and press research documents advertised reach, integration and visibility through 2015. They explain the resurgence through regulatory openings, organizational capabilities and global demand, while distinguishing promotional evidence from verified service delivery and leaving eventual dominance uncertain.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors challenge the reported demise of accounting firms’ legal ambitions.

Exact returned source excerpt

Faced with declining margins in their core audit business, the Big Five began to offer a growing arsenal of nonaudit consulting services to their audit clients, particularly in the areas of information technology and financial management.

Exact returned source excerpt

To deliver these services—and to placate regulatory officials in both law and accounting who were becoming concerned about the integration of the two disciplines—the Big Five began setting up independent legal networks that nevertheless operated under the overall umbrella of the accounting firms.

Corpus source-specific finding

The research measures public representations, not verified delivery or quality.

Exact returned source excerpt

From July 2011 to March 2012, we researched the corporate websites of PwC, Deloitte, KPMG, and EY, as well as those of affiliated law firms, collecting the following data: the location where legal services were promoted (global, regional, individual country sites); whether the websites mentioned that the firm provides legal services (if any); the web path to access legal services; the list of law practices offered; and the general textual description of the legal services.

Exact returned source excerpt

Thus, in addition to examining each of the Big Four’s main websites, we also investigated a total of 491 location sites (151 PwC, 123 Deloitte, 102 KPMG, and 115 EY).

Corpus source-specific finding

Advertised legal networks expanded beyond tax and Europe.

Exact returned source excerpt

In the spring of 2012, PwC was promoting legal services in 124 countries worldwide, Deloitte in 97 countries, KPMG in 73 countries, and EY in 29 countries.

Exact returned source excerpt

Moreover, in the majority (and in the case of EY, the overwhelming majority) of these jurisdictions, the Big Four claimed to be offering a wide range of legal services—services that go far beyond their traditional expertise in tax law to address the growing need by multinational businesses for multidisciplinary business solutions across a global network.

Corpus source-specific finding

Legal practices became more integrated and more publicly visible.

Exact returned source excerpt

As figure 3 illustrates, as of March 2012, in the majority of PwC LTs (56 percent), Deloitte LTs (65 percent), and KPMG LTs (59 percent), legal practices appeared on the web with no mention of any particular freestanding law firm or legal services firm as providing these services.

Exact returned source excerpt

Instead, the majority (54 percent) of these territories are only accessible through a “tax” or “tax and legal” core service line—notwithstanding, as we indicated above, that a much broader range of services beyond what has traditionally been considered to fall within these categories are offered.

Corpus source-specific finding

Auditor-independence rules and legal-market reforms left routes for expansion.

Exact returned source excerpt

## Gaps in the regulation of auditor independence Contrary to popular perception, section 201 of SOX did not establish an audit-only rule for the accounting firms.

Exact returned source excerpt

Instead, it provides a list of nonaudit services (NAS) that are off-limits for registered public accounting firms regarding their U.S.-listed audit clients and empowers the audit committees to preapprove the purchase of tax services and other “SOX-permissible” NAS from the auditor.

Corpus source-specific finding

Integrated organization and recurring client work underpin the proposed competitive advantage.

Exact returned source excerpt

By targeting only “bet the company” deals and litigation, however, top law firms unwittingly ceded the battle for industry expertise to the Big Four, who were only too happy to help their multinational clients with the full range of their legal problems and to treat these reoccurring legal matters as part of an overall business strategy.

Exact returned source excerpt

Leveraging their skill in process management and IT expertise across their increasingly large global footprint, the Big Four easily moved into spaces that the law firms were abandoning.

Corpus source-specific finding

Global demand and market fragmentation motivate further expansion; technology alliances add a prospective advantage.

Exact returned source excerpt

Multinational companies require consistent services around the globe, including legal services.

Exact returned source excerpt

Given their extensive experience in marshaling global resources, the Big Four are in an ideal position to meet this need, particularly in the emerging economies of Asia-Pacific, Latin America, and Africa, which are likely to see the fastest growth in the demand for legal services.

Corpus source-specific finding

Law firms’ own adaptation may strengthen the Big Four’s model, but dominance and professional consequences remain unresolved.

Exact returned source excerpt

Ironically, notwithstanding their traditional distrust of government intervention, many big law firms have followed the accountants’ lead, lobbying both their own governments and the WTO for transnational rules barring states from enforcing restrictions on the entry of foreign law firms.

Exact returned source excerpt

Indeed, by increasingly making themselves look like the Big Four in how they define and deliver legal services, large law firms may actually be hastening a world in which these global giants will no longer have to disguise their ambitions from regulators and the public.

Corpus framing of this source: The Big Four’s return to law through integrated business services

Corpus synthesis of this source: In The Reemergence of the Big Four in Law, David B. Wilkins and Maria Jose Esteban argue that integrated service delivery, global reach and business-management capabilities support the Big Four’s renewed legal ambitions. They distinguish this strategic potential from demonstrated delivery: their research primarily examines public claims, and they leave ultimate market dominance and consequences for professionalism unresolved.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Big Four rebuilt their legal networks after restrictions appeared to end their earlier expansion.

Exact returned source excerpt

After the accounting scandals in 2001 and the resulting regulation prohibiting auditors from providing legal and other nonaudit services to their audit clients, most practitioners and commentators assumed that these ambitions were dead.

Exact returned source excerpt

However, over the last decade the Big Four accounting firms have quietly rebuilt their legal networks, integrating these services into a new model of “globally integrated business solutions,” and aggressively promoting this model in important emerging economies in Asia-Pacific, Latin America, Africa, and the Middle East.

Corpus source-specific finding

Integrated organization and business expertise underpin the claimed competitive advantage.

Exact returned source excerpt

This new approach, in turn, dovetailed with the Big Four’s own organizational transformation from multidisciplinary firms, offering a variety of separate service lines, to integrated problem-solving networks with deep industry expertise.

Exact returned source excerpt

Key to the realization of these objectives has been the development by EY and its Big Four counterparts of sophisticated client management systems, as well as the positioning of risk management at the core of their strategy, spanning all countries and services lines.

Corpus source-specific finding

Global coverage, existing relationships and talent strategies support expansion.

Exact returned source excerpt

This was particularly true in Europe and other markets where indigenous firms were still very traditional, allowing the Big Four to leverage their existing relationships to move up the value chain, especially in areas at the intersection of law and business.

Exact returned source excerpt

Multinational companies require consistent services around the globe, including legal services.

Corpus source-specific finding

Legal work’s convergence with consulting could further the Big Four’s ambitions.

Exact returned source excerpt

In a prescient article published in 2002, the American legal scholar Robert Eli Rosen hypothesized that changes in the corporate market for legal services were turning both in-house counsel and outside firms into just another “consultant” whose primary task is to integrate legal knowledge into cross-functional teams to better achieve business objectives.

Exact returned source excerpt

As one of us has argued elsewhere, in the decade since Rosen’s article was published, changes in the economic and regulatory climate have only accentuated the trend he describes.

Corpus source-specific finding

Technology alliances may prepare the Big Four for disruption, but their legal-business effects remain uncertain.

Exact returned source excerpt

“We bring in the content, they bring in the technology” explained PwC’s chairman Dennis Nally.

Exact returned source excerpt

More recently, EY has established an alliance with LinkedIn designed to “help companies develop deeper and more trusted customer relationships through the use of social and data analytics.” How these moves are going to impact their global legal businesses is yet to be seen.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors qualify their conclusions with evidence limitations and regulatory barriers.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is still unclear whether any of the Big Four will be able to fulfill their ambitions of building, as Alexandru Reff, partner-in-charge of Deloitte’s Romanian tax and legal practice, confidently declared in 2013: “the largest legal network in the world.” It is, after all, far easier to promise seamless global multidisciplinary services than it is to deliver them.

Exact returned source excerpt

As we detail below, our research tracks primarily what the Big Four and others are saying about the legal services that are being delivered by their legal networks, as opposed to what they are actually doing in various markets.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors recommend closer scrutiny rather than declaring an inevitable transformation.

Exact returned source excerpt

Time will tell whether these changes will finally allow the Big Four to achieve their renewed ambition to not only be important players in the global market for legal services but reshape the very definition of that market toward a view that law is simply one part of achieving a “globally integrated business solution.” And it remains to be seen what effect the Big Four’s growing presence in law will have on larger issues of lawyer professionalism, client service, and public policy.

Exact returned source excerpt

For now, we simply conclude by urging both practitioners and commentators to pay greater attention to what these important players are doing—and, equally as important, to what law firms, clients, and regulators are, or should be, doing in response.
