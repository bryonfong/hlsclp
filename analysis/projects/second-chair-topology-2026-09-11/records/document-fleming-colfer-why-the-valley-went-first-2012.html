Corpus synthesis of this source: Fleming and colleagues frame a historical comparison of Silicon Valley and Boston around reciprocal influences between inventor networks and regional innovation. The supplied excerpts establish the debate, the authors’ proposed framing and their research design, but do not report empirical results explaining Silicon Valley’s earlier network aggregation.

Corpus framing of this source: Why the Valley Went First: Aggregation and Emergence in Regional Inventor Networks

Corpus synthesis of this source: Fleming, Colfer, Marin and McPhie combine patent-network analysis with inventor interviews to explain why Silicon Valley’s inventor network aggregated before Boston’s. They attribute the timing chiefly to institutional bridges linking Stanford, IBM and local industry, while identifying mobility, proprietary strategies, firm stability, internal labour markets and graduate retention as mechanisms shaping regional collaboration. They also qualify conventional accounts by finding little evidence that Silicon Valley’s network components were inherently denser or more robust.

Corpus source-specific finding

Silicon Valley’s earlier aggregation arose from specific university–industry bridges, not an inherently superior network structure.

Exact returned source excerpt

In the particular case at hand, Silicon Valley aggregated before Boston because Stanford graduates took employment at IBM’s Almaden Valley Labs and because IBM spon- sored a postdoctoral program that seeded the Valley with IBM patent coauthors.

Exact returned source excerpt

In contrast, MIT graduates did not take employment at GTE, DEC, Data General, or Honeywell, and none of those firms sponsored collaborative programs like that at IBM.
