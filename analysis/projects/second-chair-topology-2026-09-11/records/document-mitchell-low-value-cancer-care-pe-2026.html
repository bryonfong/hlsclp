Open topic: Healthcare ownership: Financial gains do not prove patient benefit

Corpus framing of this source: Private equity acquisition showed no increase in five measured low-value cancer treatments

Corpus synthesis of this source: Use of Low-Value Cancer Care in Practices Acquired by Private Equity Firms examines Medicare data from 2015–2021. The authors find reductions in two measured treatments and no statistically significant differences in three others, contrary to their financial-incentive hypothesis. They qualify the findings as associative, limited to five services, and compatible with other strategies for increasing practice profitability.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors test a financial-incentive hypothesis rather than assume private equity increases low-value care.

Exact returned source excerpt

Private Equity (PE) firms commonly invest in US healthcare practices, seeking to generate financial returns by consolidating care delivery,1 reducing overhead costs, and increasing revenue.2 While PE acquisition may generate operational efficiencies and provide capital for struggling practices, it has been associated with staffing reductions, increased prices,3–8 and selling of real estate assets,9 which may negatively impact care quality.10 The proportion of healthcare practices owned by PE firms has increased for many specialties.3 Oncology has recently become a target of PE investment, with approximately 10% of medical oncologists and 15% of radiation oncologists practicing in PE-owned clinics by 2022.11 PE ownership in oncology has grown rapidly with limited evidence on utilization and quality.

Exact returned source excerpt

Cancer treatments that are clinically unnecessary or have lower-cost alternatives result in avoidable costs and worse patient outcomes (through both drug toxicity and financial toxicity) but may still be lucrative to the provider.

Corpus source-specific finding

The study measures five specific low-value treatments in a large Medicare cohort.

Exact returned source excerpt

We used 100% traditional Medicare (TM) claims and Medicare Advantage (MA) national encounter data from 2015 through 2021.

Exact returned source excerpt

We included Medicare beneficiaries with a new cancer diagnosis from January 1, 2016 to June 30, 2020, and no cancer diagnosis or treatment claim for a ≥1 year washout period (Supplementary Methods A1).

Corpus source-specific finding

The analysis links ownership exposure to practice addresses and uses adjusted difference-in-differences comparisons.

Exact returned source excerpt

13 Physicians’ historical practice addresses were then mapped to those identified as PE-acquired (data from Pitchbook and other public sources, including press releases).11 Using dates of both PE acquisition and practice address changes, we identified 1) physicians who ever practiced at a PE-acquired address; 2) the time intervals during which they were and were not at a PE-acquired address.

Exact returned source excerpt

We estimated the association of PE acquisition and low-value treatment within each of the five sub-cohorts separately.

Corpus source-specific finding

PE acquisition was associated with lower use of two treatments and no significant differences in the other three.

Exact returned source excerpt

After multivariable adjustment from the DID estimation, the corresponding prevalence differences were: GCSF, −2.8%-points (−5.5 to −0.2); denosumab, +3.7%-points (−7.3 to 14.7); nab-paclitaxel, −0.7%-points (−3.9 to 1.5); bevacizumab, −6.5%-points (−12.5 to −0.4); biologics, −2.9%-points (−6.0 to 0.2) (Table 1).

Exact returned source excerpt

PE acquisition was not associated with differences in use of three of the low-value treatments and was associated with reductions in two low-value treatments (GCSF for low-risk chemotherapy and bevacizumab for ovarian cancer).

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors propose alternative explanations and recommend studying other profitability strategies.

Exact returned source excerpt

For two low-value services (GCSF and bevacizumab), use appeared unexpectedly lower in PE-acquired firms, further supporting that increased drug utilization may not be a common firm strategy.

Exact returned source excerpt

This finding differs from prior work suggesting that PE acquisition impacts care delivery: PE-acquired ophthalmology practices had both increased volume of intravitreal injections and increased cost per procedure due to substitution of more-expensive drugs.16 The difference may be that services such as intravitreal injections are standardized, high-volume procedures such that substituting higher-priced drugs might substantially impact overall practice revenue, whereas the five low-value treatments we studied are relatively uncommon and therefore less likely to receive attention by practice ownership.

Corpus source-specific finding

The findings do not establish causality or describe all cancer care.

Corpus framing of this source: Private-equity acquisition and low-value cancer treatment use

Corpus synthesis of this source: The study examines whether private-equity acquisition of oncology practices changed the use of five low-value cancer treatments among Medicare patients. It finds no increase across the treatments studied, considers why this differs from expectations and earlier evidence in ophthalmology, and stresses that the observational results have limited scope and do not establish causality.

Corpus source-specific finding

Corpus source-specific finding

Interpretation of the unexpected result

Exact returned source excerpt

This result may be unexpected considering PE firms’ incentives and strategy, which is to increase the profitability of acquired practices prior to “exit” or resale.

Exact returned source excerpt

The five low-value treatments we evaluated are all revenue-generating services, and their use (and overuse) would be aligned with the goal of increasing profitability.

Alternative mechanisms and comparison with prior research

Exact returned source excerpt

Studies of non-oncology medical settings have found that PE firms may use other mechanisms besides direct involvement in care delivery – such as consolidation, pricing, and staff reductions – to increase profitability.3–8 Further study is needed to assess whether these strategies are employed in oncology acquisitions as well.
