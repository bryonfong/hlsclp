Builds onAI instruction: Timing divides educational reform

Open topic: AI instruction: Timing divides educational reform

Corpus framing of this source: Chesney’s law-school AI strategy: skills, assessment integrity and educational rigor

Corpus synthesis of this source: In his June 16, 2026 letter, Texas Law Dean Bobby Chesney argues that AI training should complement traditional legal education. He outlines curricular and access initiatives, reports changes to assessments, and distinguishes AI’s educational benefits from its potential to weaken independent learning. His case rests on institutional experience, conversations with alumni and employers, and qualified predictions rather than measured learning outcomes.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI competence and traditional legal judgment are complementary educational goals.

Exact returned source excerpt

Some discussions of this topic have a largely negative framing, as if there is an irreconcilable tension between the traditional pedagogical goals of legal education and the emergent need to train students to take effective advantage of AI capabilities.

Exact returned source excerpt

But these goals are not in a zero-sum relationship.

Corpus source-specific finding

Schools must address three distinct challenges and continually revise their response.

Exact returned source excerpt

• The skills challenge: What AI-related knowledge and skills should we impart to students (and how should we accomplish this)?

Exact returned source excerpt

• The assessments challenge: Given ubiquitous student access to AI (whether or not we provide access to particular tools), what steps should we take to preserve the integrity of our assessment measures?

Exact returned source excerpt

• Understanding the options for accessing various models and tools, and developing judgment about how to select an appropriate path for a particular task.

Corpus framing of this source: AI and Legal Education: a dual commitment to AI readiness and educational rigor

Corpus synthesis of this source: Robert M. Chesney argues that law schools should prepare students to use AI wisely while preserving the expert judgment developed through traditional legal education. He frames the task as three linked challenges—skills, assessment integrity and educational rigor—requiring continual reassessment. He supports targeted educational uses such as practice-exam generation and Socratic tutoring, while qualifying that AI cannot replace the mentoring and authoritative course-specific guidance available from professors.
