Corpus framing of this source: Public utility thinking as a response to private platform power

Corpus synthesis of this source: The New Utilities argues that major internet platforms increasingly function as social and economic infrastructure. It proposes public utility thinking as a way to identify and address the private power this role creates, while acknowledging the benefits of consolidated platform control. The supplied passage offers a conceptual argument rather than empirical findings or a detailed regulatory programme.

Corpus framing of this source: Rahman’s modern public-utility framework for infrastructural private power

Corpus synthesis of this source: K. Sabeel Rahman revives the public-utility tradition as a portable framework for identifying and regulating concentrated private control over socially essential infrastructure. He defines infrastructural sectors through scale effects, broad downstream uses, and user vulnerability, then develops three adaptable tools—firewalls, public obligations, and public options—and applies them to broadband, finance, and digital platforms. He treats the framework as a complement to antitrust and corporate governance, not a return to rigid rate regulation or a complete policy blueprint.

Corpus source-specific finding

Develops a three-part regulatory toolkit

Exact returned source excerpt

Drawing from these disparate debates over net neutrality and TBTF financial firms, I extract four key elements of a twenty-first century framework for public utility regulation: firewalling core necessities away from behaviors and practices that might contaminate the basic provision of these goods and services-including through structural limits on the corporate organization and form of firms that provide infrastructural goods; imposing public obligations on infrastructural firms, whether negative obligations to prevent discrimination or unfair disparities in prices or positive obligations to proactively provide equal, affordable, and accessible services to under-served constituencies; and creating public options, state-chartered, cheaper, basic versions of these services that would offer an alternative to exploitative private control in markets otherwise immune to competitive pressures.

Exact returned source excerpt

It also suggests that regulation can and should be tailored to meet the nature of the problem at hand: public utility strategies can be dialed up (as in the case of a full public option) or dialed down (such as through more light-touch regulatory obligations of nondiscrimination).
