Corpus framing of this source: Downturn-related employment cuts across legal employers

Corpus synthesis of this source: After the JD III reports respondents’ accounts of business contraction, staffing cuts and compensation changes across legal employment settings. It highlights large-firm layoffs and slower partnership promotion, while documenting substantial pressures elsewhere. The report suggests that protecting partner profits, alongside weaker demand, likely intensified large firms’ incentives to cut staff.

Corpus framing of this source: After the JD III: Midcareer mobility, sector shifts and persistent inequalities

Corpus synthesis of this source: After the JD III: Third Results from a National Study of Legal Careers follows the careers of lawyers who entered practice around 2000. Its principal contribution is descriptive evidence of slowing mobility alongside continued movement away from private firms, persistent earnings and promotion inequalities, and generally high career satisfaction. The report offers tentative explanations involving career stage and the recession, while warning that changing respondents and differing measures complicate comparisons.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus framing of this source: Racial and ethnic differences in legal careers across the After the JD survey waves

Corpus synthesis of this source: After the JD III documents the demographic composition and changing practice settings of lawyers who entered practice around 2000. It reports distinct racial and ethnic patterns in declining law-firm employment and growing public-sector and business employment, while cautioning that changes in survey participation explain some differences across waves.

Corpus source-specific finding

The third-wave sample combined near gender parity with a predominantly White demographic composition.

Exact returned source excerpt

Nelson The After the JD sample reflects the growing diversity of the American legal profession, but its demographic composition also shows that the profession remains an occupation in which most members are White.

Exact returned source excerpt

As Table 2.1 shows, among lawyers interviewed in the third wave of the study, almost exactly half (50.4%) were women and four fifths (82.8%) were White.

Corpus source-specific finding

Cross-wave comparisons partly reflect changing respondents, not just changes in careers.

Exact returned source excerpt

Comparisons across waves, such as those in Table 2.1, are, unless otherwise noted, based on comparing responses from whoever answered the survey in a specific wave.

Exact returned source excerpt

Thus, some of the differences across waves reflect the fact that a different set of respondents participated in the three waves of the study.

Corpus source-specific finding

Law-firm employment declined unevenly across racial and ethnic groups.

Exact returned source excerpt

In Wave 1, Black respondents had the smallest share of lawyers in law firms, and after the numbers leveled off at Wave 2, at Wave 3, they again had the lowest share of lawyers in private firms, with just over one third of Black lawyers working in this sector.

Exact returned source excerpt

The proportion of Hispanic lawyers in private firms in Wave 3 declined only marginally compared to Wave 2, such that their representation in this sector comes closest to approximating the representation of Whites in this sector (45.6% for Hispanics and 50.2% for Whites).

Firm size further differentiated the reported employment changes.

Exact returned source excerpt

For Black lawyers, the greatest declines came in small and large firms while Hispanic lawyers experienced a different pattern:

Exact returned source excerpt

They showed modest declines in small practice settings and more substantial declines in medium-sized to large firms.

Corpus source-specific finding

Government and public-sector employment grew, including an unexpectedly large shift among Asian lawyers.

Exact returned source excerpt

Government and Public Sector: While working in a law firm became less common for AJD3 lawyers, working in government became more common.

Exact returned source excerpt

At the very start of their careers, Black lawyers were over-represented in government and public sector positions, and while there was some change at Wave 2, by Wave 3, Black lawyers were again over-represented in this sector (42%).

Corpus source-specific finding

Business employment continued growing across every racial and ethnic group.

Exact returned source excerpt

Business: The growth in the business sector (encompassing positions practicing law as well as not practicing law) that we witnessed at Wave 2 continued for all racial/ethnic groups.

Exact returned source excerpt

Across the sample, Asian (27.5%) lawyers are most likely to be working in business, with Hispanics least likely to be working in this sector (15.3%).
