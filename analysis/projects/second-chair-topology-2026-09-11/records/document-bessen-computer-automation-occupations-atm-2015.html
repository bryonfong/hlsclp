Corpus framing of this source: Task automation, occupational employment and the challenge of learning new skills

Corpus synthesis of this source: How Computer Automation Affects Occupations: Technology, Jobs, and Skills models occupations as bundles of tasks and explains why faster task performance need not reduce employment. The author combines a stylised production model with historical and published evidence, makes employment effects conditional on demand and substitution, and distinguishes present skill-learning difficulties from possible future job losses.

Corpus framing of this source: Computer automation reallocates jobs and raises skill demands without substantial net job loss

Corpus synthesis of this source: James Bessen’s 2015 paper, How Computer Automation Affects Occupations: Technology, Jobs, and Skills, analyses US occupational changes from 1980–2013. It links computer use to faster growth in adopting occupations, offsetting displacement elsewhere, and greater within-occupation wage inequality. Bessen emphasises costly occupational learning rather than technological unemployment, while acknowledging causal, measurement and scope limitations and the possibility of future job losses.

Corpus synthesis of this source: James Bessen’s 2015 working paper develops an occupational model and analyses detailed occupations since 1980. It argues that computerisation reallocates employment and changes skill demand rather than causing significant net technological unemployment or job polarisation, while contributing to wage inequality within occupations.
