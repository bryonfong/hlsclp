Corpus source-specific finding

Construction process The dataset was manually created to test an LLM’s ability to determine whether a contract is governed by the UCC or by the common law.

Exact returned source excerpt

The dataset is composed of 100 descriptions of simple contracts such as “Alice and Bob enter into a contract for Alice to sell her bike to Bob for $50” (UCC) and “Aria pays Owen $100 to mount a television on the wall of her living room” (common law).

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors explain why the governing-law distinction matters through differences in contract rules.

Exact returned source excerpt

The UCC only requires that a goods contract include the good being sold and the quantity.

Exact returned source excerpt

If any other term is missing from the contract (such as price or delivery), it will be filled in by UCC default rules.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors frame the task as a basic legal-reasoning and subject-matter recognition benchmark.

Exact returned source excerpt

First, it provides a measure of an LLM’s legal reasoning ability relative to a human lawyer (who would almost certainly score a 100% on the task).

Exact returned source excerpt

Second, it demonstrates an LLM’s ability to determine the subject matter of a legal text, which has implications for the use of LLMs for legal tasks far beyond contract classification.

Corpus source-specific finding

Contract sorting and mixed-purpose classification are proposed applications, not demonstrated outcomes.

Exact returned source excerpt

Third, this task could prove useful in the context of contract lifecycle management (CLM) in which a CLM software product could automatically sort contracts by subject matter for review purposes.

Exact returned source excerpt

Fourth, while the sample contracts in the dataset were simple and easily identifiable as either UCC or common law contracts, real-world mixed purpose contracts can be difficult to classify and sometimes generate costly litigation.
