SupportsPredictive coding: Mature automation still needs transparency

Open topic: Predictive coding: Mature automation still needs transparency

Corpus framing of this source: Da Silva Moore: conditional judicial approval of predictive coding

Corpus synthesis of this source: Judge Andrew J. Peck’s 2012 opinion accepts predictive coding for appropriate electronic discovery, grounding approval in comparative accuracy, proportionality, transparency and quality control. It distinguishes agreement to use the technology from disputes over implementation and offers practical guidance on training, testing and staged discovery.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court approved predictive coding in this case, not its compulsory use in every case.

Exact returned source excerpt

In this case, the Court determined that the use of predictive coding was appropriate considering: (1) the parties’ agreement, (2) the vast amount of ESI to be reviewed (over three million documents), (3) the superiority of computer-assisted review to the available alternatives _(i.e.,_ linear manual review or keyword searches), (4) the need for cost effectiveness and proportionality under Rule 26(b)(2)(C), and (5) the transparent process proposed by MSL.

Exact returned source excerpt

To correct the many blogs about this case, initiated by a press release from plaintiffs’ vendor — the Court did not order the parties to use predictive coding.

Corpus source-specific finding

Peck rejects exhaustive manual review as an inherently superior standard, drawing on published comparative studies.

Exact returned source excerpt

Linear manual review is simply too expensive where, as here, there are over three million emails to review.

Exact returned source excerpt

Herb Roitblatt, Anne Kershaw, and Patrick Oot of the Electronic Discovery Institute conducted an empirical assessment to “answer the question of whether there was a benefit to engaging in a traditional human review or whether computer systems could be relied on to produce comparable results,” and concluded that “[o]n every measure, the performance of the two computer systems was at least as accurate (measured against the original review) as that of human re-review.” Herbert L.

Corpus source-specific finding

Predictive coding shifts work toward experienced lawyers’ training and selective review.

Exact returned source excerpt

> Unlike manual review, where the review is done by the most junior staff, computer-assisted coding involves a senior partner (or [small] team) who review and code a “seed set” of documents.

Exact returned source excerpt

As the senior reviewer continues to code more sample documents, the computer predicts the reviewer’s coding.

Corpus source-specific finding

Defensibility depends on demonstrable process and results, not merely the software’s underlying science.

Exact returned source excerpt

2011, at 25, 29.[2](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8782929/moore-v-publicis-groupe/#fn1) My article further explained my belief that _Daubert_ would not apply to the results of using predictive coding, but that in any challenge to its use, this Judge would be interested in both the process used and the results: > [I]f the use of predictive coding is challenged in a case before me, I will want to know what was done and why that produced defensible results.

Exact returned source excerpt

I may be less interested in the science behind the “black box” of the vendor’s software than in whether it produced responsive documents with reasonably high recall and high precision.

Corpus source-specific finding

The protocol makes training and transparency concrete without removing privilege protections.

Exact returned source excerpt

at 54.)[5](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8782929/moore-v-publicis-groupe/#fn4) _The Predictive Coding Protocol_ The parties agreed to use a 95% confidence level (plus or minus two percent) to create a random sample of the entire email collection; that sample of 2,399 documents will be reviewed to determine relevant (and not relevant) documents for a “seed set” to use to train the predictive coding software.

Exact returned source excerpt

at 62.) MSL suggested that since it had agreed to provide all 2,399 documents (and MSL’s coding of them) to plaintiffs for their review, plaintiffs can code them for the new issue tags, and MSL will incorporate that coding into the system.

Corpus source-specific finding

Stopping decisions should follow tested results; staged discovery can control costs.

Exact returned source excerpt

First, it is unlikely that courts will be able to determine or approve a party’s proposal as to when review and production can stop until the computer-assisted review software has been trained and the results are quality control verified.

Exact returned source excerpt

While cost is a factor under Rule 26(b)(2)(C), it cannot be considered in isolation from the results of the predictive coding process and the amount at issue in the litigation.

Corpus source-specific finding

Cooperation and informed search design remain essential regardless of technology.

Exact returned source excerpt

> Of course, the best approach to the use of computer-assisted coding is to follow the Sedona Cooperation Proclamation model.

Exact returned source excerpt

Advise opposing counsel that you plan to use computer-assisted coding and seek agreement; if you cannot, consider whether to abandon predictive coding for that case or go to the court for advance approval.

Corpus framing of this source: Da Silva Moore: conditional judicial approval of predictive coding in discovery

Corpus synthesis of this source: Magistrate Judge Andrew Peck approves computer-assisted document review in this 2012 case, grounding his decision in comparative research, discovery costs and proportionality. He stresses human training, quality control and cooperation rather than wholesale replacement of lawyers, and leaves stopping decisions dependent on actual results.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court approves predictive coding for appropriate cases, not as a universal solution.

Exact returned source excerpt

In this case, the Court determined that the use of predictive coding was appropriate considering: (1) the parties’ agreement, (2) the vast amount of ESI to be reviewed (over three million documents), (3) the superiority of computer-assisted review to the available alternatives _(i.e.,_ linear manual review or keyword searches), (4) the need for cost effectiveness and proportionality under Rule 26(b)(2)(C), and (5) the transparent process proposed by MSL.

Exact returned source excerpt

Computer-assisted review now can be considered judicially-approved for use in appropriate cases.

Corpus source-specific finding

Comparative studies undermine the premise that exhaustive human review is the best available method.

Exact returned source excerpt

Moreover, while some lawyers still consider manual review to be the “gold standard,” that is a myth, as statistics clearly show that computerized searches are at least as accurate, if not more so, than manual review.

Exact returned source excerpt

Herb Roitblatt, Anne Kershaw, and Patrick Oot of the Electronic Discovery Institute conducted an empirical assessment to “answer the question of whether there was a benefit to engaging in a traditional human review or whether computer systems could be relied on to produce comparable results,” and concluded that “[o]n every measure, the performance of the two computer systems was at least as accurate (measured against the original review) as that of human re-review.” Herbert L.

Corpus source-specific finding

Human inconsistency supports automation, but technology-assisted processes still vary in quality.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Roitblatt, Kershaw, Oot article noted that "[t]he level of agreement among human reviewers is not strikingly high,” around 70-75%.

Exact returned source excerpt

They identify two sources for this variability: fatigue ("A document that they [the reviewers] might have categorized as responsive when they were more attentive might then be categorized [when the reviewer is distracted or fatigued] as non-responsive or vice versa.”), and differences in "strategic judgment.” _Id._ at 77-78.

Corpus source-specific finding

Predictive coding reallocates human work toward expert training and quality control.

Exact returned source excerpt

> Unlike manual review, where the review is done by the most junior staff, computer-assisted coding involves a senior partner (or [small] team) who review and code a “seed set” of documents.

Exact returned source excerpt

(Or, the computer codes some documents and asks the senior reviewer for feedback.) > When the system’s predictions and the reviewer’s coding sufficiently coincide, the system has learned enough to make confident predictions for the remaining documents.

Corpus source-specific finding

Keyword searches remain useful, but poorly informed terms can generate costly false positives.

Exact returned source excerpt

/opinion/8782929/moore-v-publicis-groupe/#191)many cases, however, the way lawyers choose keywords is the equivalent of the child’s game of “Go Fish.”[13](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8782929/moore-v-publicis-groupe/#fn12) The requesting party guesses which keywords might produce evidence to support its case without having much, if any, knowledge of the responding party’s “cards” (i.e., the terminology used by the responding party’s custodians).

Exact returned source excerpt

In this case, for example, a keyword search for “training” resulted in 165,208 hits; Da Silva Moore’s name resulted in 201,179 hits; “bonus” resulted in 40,756 hits; “compensation” resulted in 55,602 hits; and “diversity” resulted in 38,315 hits.

Corpus source-specific finding

Proportionality requires tested results, not perfection or a cost-only stopping rule.

Exact returned source excerpt

The goal is for the review method to result in higher recall and higher precision than another review method, at a cost proportionate to the “value” of the case.

Exact returned source excerpt

While this Court recognizes that computer-assisted review is not perfect, the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure do not require perfection.

Corpus source-specific finding

Counsel should stage discovery and cooperate using client and custodian knowledge.

Exact returned source excerpt

Second, staging of discovery by starting with the most likely to be relevant sources (including custodians), without prejudice to the requesting party seeking more after conclusion of that first stage review, is a way to control discovery costs.

Exact returned source excerpt

If staging requires a longer discovery period, most judges should be willing to grant such an extension.

Corpus framing of this source: Da Silva Moore: Judicial acceptance and supervised implementation of predictive coding

Corpus synthesis of this source: Magistrate Judge Andrew J. Peck’s opinion recognizes computer-assisted review as an acceptable ESI-search method in appropriate cases and supplies a detailed, court-supervised protocol. It grounds acceptance in comparative research, proportionality, transparency and quality control rather than technological perfection, while preserving plaintiffs’ objections and future challenges to the process.

The Court recognizes computer-assisted review as acceptable in appropriate cases, but does not endorse a particular vendor or make its use universally mandatory.
