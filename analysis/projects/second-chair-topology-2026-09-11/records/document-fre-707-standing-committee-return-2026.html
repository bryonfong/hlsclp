Open topic: AI evidence: Reliability differs from deepfake authenticity

Corpus framing of this source: Evidence Rules Committee revises AI reliability safeguards and defers action pending further study

Corpus synthesis of this source: The May 17, 2026 report and attached draft minutes distinguish the reliability of acknowledged AI output from the authenticity of suspected deepfakes. The Committee prefers a revised Rule 707 that ordinarily requires an expert foundation but allows exceptional alternative proof. It postpones further publication while seeking technical and practitioner evidence on both AI proposals; neither proposal represents an adopted rule.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI output can present a reliability gap even when its origin is acknowledged.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Committee has determined that there are two evidentiary challenges raised by AI: (1) evidence that is an acknowledged product of AI, such as machine learning, which would be subject to Rule 702 if propounded by an expert witness; and (2) evidence that is not authentic because it is a difficult-to-detect deepfake.

Exact returned source excerpt

But Rule 702 is not clearly applicable if the AI output is admitted without any expert testimony — either directly (for example, under Rule 902(13)) or by way of a lay witness (such as where facial recognition testimony is proffered by a person who merely “pressed the button” and has no idea how the system works).

Corpus source-specific finding

Proposed Rule 707 would apply expert-equivalent reliability requirements, not create a lower standard for AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

If evidence is a product of artificial intelligence and is 4 offered without an expert witness, but would be subject to Rule 702 if 5 testified to by a witness, the proponent must establish that the evidence: 6 (1) will help the trier of fact; 7 (2) is based on sufficient facts or data; 8 (3) is the product of reliable principles and methods; and 9 (4) reflects a reliable application of the principles and methods to the 10 facts of the case.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Committee concluded that amending Rule 702 itself would not be workable, for two reasons: (1) that Rule was just amended in 2023; (2) it is a rule of general applicability, and a separate subdivision dealing with machine evidence would be inappropriately specific and difficult to draft.

Corpus framing of this source: Proposed federal evidence rules for AI output and deepfakes

Corpus synthesis of this source: The Advisory Committee distinguishes reliability problems in acknowledged AI output from authenticity problems posed by deepfakes. It develops Rule 707 to apply expert-evidence reliability safeguards when AI output enters without an expert, while exploring—but postponing—Rule 901(c) for deepfake challenges pending further technical and practitioner input.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Committee identifies two distinct evidentiary problems requiring different legal analysis.

Exact returned source excerpt

Artificial Intelligence For the past three years, the Committee has been researching and investigating whether the existing Evidence Rules are sufficient to assure that evidence created by artificial intelligence (“AI”) will be properly regulated for reliability and authenticity.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Committee has determined that there are two evidentiary challenges raised by AI: (1) evidence that is an acknowledged product of AI, such as machine learning, which would be subject to Rule 702 if propounded by an expert witness; and (2) evidence that is not authentic because it is a difficult-to-detect deepfake.

Corpus source-specific finding

Rule 707 would close a gap where AI output resembles expert testimony but enters without an expert.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Committee’s view is that the reliability issues attendant to AI are akin to those raised by experts under Rule 702.

Exact returned source excerpt

But Rule 702 is not clearly applicable if the AI output is admitted without any expert testimony — either directly (for example, under Rule 902(13)) or by way of a lay witness (such as where facial recognition testimony is proffered by a person who merely “pressed the button” and has no idea how the system works).
