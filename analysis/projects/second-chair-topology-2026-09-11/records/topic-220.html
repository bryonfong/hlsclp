Japan governance: Voluntary cooperation serves innovation-first policy

Corpus synthesis: Combines central coordination and investment with low burdens and reputational accountability, while existing data and copyright law remain applicable.

Native topic findings

  1. Japan offers an innovation-first model that formalises AI governance while retaining voluntary private-sector cooperation.
  2. The framework couples low regulatory burdens with public investment and central coordination, but its economic benefits remain prospective.
  3. Accountability relies on executive governance, guidance and possible reputational pressure rather than explicit penalties under the Act.
  4. The main disagreement concerns regulatory instruments, not the stated goals of responsible AI.
  5. An innovation-oriented AI statute does not remove existing copyright and personal-data constraints.
  6. Businesses should treat the present framework as evolving, and distinguish legal reporting from forecasts.

Japan's Emerging Framework for Responsible AI: Legislation, Guidelines and Guidance

Regulators, courts & professional bodies · Adoption at scale (2025)

Corpus framing of this source: Japan’s layered AI governance: voluntary strategy, organisational accountability and legal adaptation

Corpus synthesis of this source: In their July 2025 article, Kensuke Inoue and Chika Kamata describe Japan’s AI framework through legislation, business guidelines and interpretations of existing law. They argue that this flexible approach balances innovation with accountability, while distinguishing current voluntary measures from possible binding regulation and proposed changes to data-consent requirements.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors present three complementary pillars rather than a single prescriptive AI regime.

Exact returned source excerpt

Instead of rigid regulation, Japan relies on three pillars: (1) the non-binding Act on the Promotion of Research, Development and Utilisation of Artificial Intelligence-Related Technologies (the ‘AI Promotion Act’), which sets the strategic direction; (2) the 2024 AI Business Operator Guidelines (the ‘AI Guidelines for Business’), which translate principles into practice; and (3) guidance on the interpretation of existing statutes, such as the Copyright Act and the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI) to address issues raised by generative AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

This integrated framework balances innovation with accountability and international compatibility.

The AI Promotion Act coordinates national AI development through strategic priorities and shared responsibilities.

Exact returned source excerpt

# The AI Promotion Act: a strategic framework for AI promotion On 28 May 2025, the AI Promotion Act was passed by the Diet (Japan’s national legislature) into law.

Exact returned source excerpt

The AI Promotion Act rests on four core principles: viewing AI as a strategic asset, promoting industrial use, mitigating the risks through transparency and actively contributing to international AI norms.

The authors describe the Act as non-binding but anticipate possible stronger regulation.

Exact returned source excerpt

It does not create enforceable rights or duties, but serves as a soft-law instrument, encouraging voluntary compliance through political signalling and administrative coordination.

Exact returned source excerpt

This mirrors Japan’s broader administrative tradition, according to which ‘regulation by guidance’ is often preferred over punitive enforcement.

Business guidelines make ethical AI an executive governance responsibility.

# The AI Guidelines for Business: soft law in practice Published by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) and the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC) in April 2024, the AI Guidelines for Business respond to global discussions, including those that took place during the 2023 G7 Hiroshima AI Summit.

Exact returned source excerpt

Structured according to three tiers, the Guidelines define the foundational values (human dignity, inclusion, sustainability), set out ten cross-sector principles (eg, fairness, privacy, safety) and offer useful tools, such as checklists and case studies.

Voluntary guidelines may influence legal interpretation, commercial scrutiny and industry standards.

Exact returned source excerpt

Although non-binding, the Guidelines may influence court interpretations, administrative guidance, procurement policies and environmental, social and governance (ESG) assessments.

Exact returned source excerpt

For businesses, they serve as a benchmark for good governance, particularly as AI becomes increasingly subject to scrutiny by regulators, investors and the public.

# Japanese copyright law and generative AI In May 2024, the Agency for Cultural Affairs, which is the agency responsible for copyright law and policy, published a document entitled the ‘General Understanding on AI and Copyright in Japan’ to clarify the interpretation of relevant copyright statutes in the context of generative AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

Article 30-4 permits non-expressive uses, such as data analysis for training purposes, without the need for authorisation by the rightsholder, so long as the output does not replicate the expressive works.

Understanding Japan's AI Promotion Act: An 'Innovation-First' Blueprint for AI Regulation

Access to justice & civil society · Adoption at scale (2025)

Corpus framing of this source: Japan’s innovation-first AI law: cooperative governance and contrasting regulatory models

Corpus synthesis of this source: Dominic Paulger’s Future of Privacy Forum analysis characterises Japan’s AI Promotion Act as a statutory framework that preserves voluntary, innovation-oriented governance. He explains its investment rationale, institutional structure and enforcement limits, contrasts it with EU and South Korean regulation, and presents regulatory competition as an uncertain prospect rather than a demonstrated outcome.

Corpus source-specific finding

Japan formalised AI governance without abandoning its soft-law approach.

Exact returned source excerpt

In a landmark move, on May 28, 2025, Japan’s Parliament approved the “Act on the Promotion of Research and Development and the Utilization of AI-Related Technologies” (人工知能関連技術の研究開発及び活用の推進に関する法律案要綱) (**AI Promotion Act**, or **Act**), making Japan the second major economy in the Asia-Pacific (**APAC**) region to enact comprehensive AI legislation.

Exact returned source excerpt

Most provisions of the Act (except Chapters 3 and 4, and Articles 3 and 4 of its Supplementary Provisions) took effect on June 4, 2025, marking a significant transition from Japan’s soft-law, guideline-based approach to AI governance to a formal legislative framework.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Act seeks to reverse weak AI investment and adoption by encouraging experimentation.

Exact returned source excerpt

The Act implements the Japanese government’s ambition, outlined in a 2024 whitepaper, to make Japan the world’s “most AI-friendly country.” The Act is specifically designed to create an environment that encourages investment and experimentation by deliberately avoiding the imposition of stringent rules or penalties that could stifle development.

Exact returned source excerpt

This initiative is a direct response to low rates of AI adoption and investment in Japan.

Corpus source-specific finding

Broad coverage and five principles give the framework flexibility.

Exact returned source excerpt

This term refers to technologies that replicate human intellectual capabilities like cognition, inference, and judgment through artificial means, as well as the systems that use them.

Exact returned source excerpt

Notably, the law proposes a unique approach to defining the scope of covered AI technologies and does not adopt the OECD definition of an AI system which served as inspiration for that in the EU AI Act.

Corpus source-specific finding

Central government coordination anchors a whole-of-society promotion programme.

Exact returned source excerpt

The centerpiece of the new governance structure established under the Act is the establishment of a centralized **AI Strategy Headquarters** within Japan’s Cabinet.

Exact returned source excerpt

Chaired by the Prime Minister and including all other Cabinet ministers as members, this body ensures a whole-of-government, coordinated approach to AI policy.

Corpus source-specific finding

Enforcement relies on cooperation and possible reputational pressure, not explicit penalties.

Exact returned source excerpt

**The AI Promotion Act contains no explicit penalties, financial or otherwise, for non-compliance with its requirements or, more broadly, for misusing AI.** Instead, its enforcement power rests on a unique cooperative and reputational model.

Exact returned source excerpt

- **A “Duty to Cooperate”:** The sole direct obligation imposed on private sector businesses is to “endeavor to cooperate” with measures implemented by the government.

Corpus source-specific finding

The comparison distinguishes Japanese promotion, EU precaution and South Korean targeted regulation.

Exact returned source excerpt

However, Japan’s AI Promotion Act does not impose any direct obligations on private actors and does not include a “risk” or “high-impact” classification of AI technologies.

Exact returned source excerpt

The EU AI Act has the broadest and most detailed scope, categorizing all AI systems into four risk levels, with strict requirements for high-risk and outright prohibitions for unacceptable risk systems, in addition to specific obligations for General Purpose AI (**GPAI**) models.

Corpus source-specific finding

Different enforcement tools coexist with shared responsible-AI objectives.

Exact returned source excerpt

In terms of enforcement powers, Japan’s AI Promotion Law notably lacks any penalties for noncompliance or misuse of AI more broadly.

Exact returned source excerpt

South Korea’s AI Framework Act, by contrast, has enforcement powers, including fines and corrective orders, but its financial penalties are comparatively lower than those in the EU’s AI Act.

Corpus source-specific finding

Low immediate compliance burdens do not settle the longer-term regulatory or investment outcome.

Exact returned source excerpt

**The future of AI regulation: A new regional and global landscape** The distinctly “light-touch” approach to AI regulation in Japan suggests a minimal compliance burden for organizations in the immediate term.

Exact returned source excerpt

However, the AI Promotion Act is arguably the beginning, not the end, of the conversation, as the forthcoming Basic AI Plan has the potential to introduce a wide range of possible initiatives.
