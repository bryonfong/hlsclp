Open topic: Lawyer accountability: Discipline and liability remain separate

Corpus framing of this source: Hizey v. Carpenter: separating disciplinary rules from malpractice liability

Corpus synthesis of this source: In Hizey v. Carpenter, 119 Wn.2d 251, 830 P.2d 646 (1992), the Washington Supreme Court affirmed the exclusion of explicit references to Washington’s CPR and RPC from malpractice testimony and jury instructions. The opinion distinguishes disciplinary obligations from civil duties while allowing experts to draw on ethical requirements when independently establishing a breach of the legal standard of care.

Corpus source-specific finding

An ethics-rule violation neither creates a malpractice cause of action nor supplies evidence of malpractice as such.

Exact returned source excerpt

Because the CPR and RPC explicitly, and in what we deem to be clear and unambiguous language, disclaim any intent to create civil liability standards, we refuse to hold their violation creates a cause of action for malpractice.

Exact returned source excerpt

The result of such holdings, with which we concur, has been that breach of an ethics rule provides only a public, _e.g.,_ disciplinary, remedy and not a private remedy.

Corpus source-specific finding

Malpractice requires proof of a distinct, circumstance-specific legal duty and resulting harm.

Exact returned source excerpt

_The CPR and RPC are ill suited for use in the malpractice arena._ lb establish a claim for legal malpractice, a plaintiff must prove the following elements: (1) The existence of an attorney-client relationship which gives rise to a duty of care on the part of the attorney to the client; (2) an act or omission by the attorney in breach of the duty of care; (3) damage to the client; and (4) proximate causation between [*261](http://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1162803/hizey-v-carpenter/#261) the attorney's breach of the duty and the damage incurred.

Exact returned source excerpt

To comply with the duty of care, an attorney must exercise the degree of care, skill, diligence, and knowledge commonly possessed and exercised by a reasonable, careful, and prudent lawyer in the practice of law in this jurisdiction.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court rejects the analogy between ethics rules and negligence-related statutes or regulations.

Exact returned source excerpt

In furtherance of their argument, plaintiffs analogize the CPR and RPC to statutes or administrative regulations, the violation of which produces evidence of negligence or, in some jurisdictions, negligence per se.

Exact returned source excerpt

The CPR and RPC are not statutes or administrative regulations.

Corpus source-specific finding

Institutional policy supports keeping discipline and civil liability separate.

Exact returned source excerpt

[*263](http://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1162803/hizey-v-carpenter/#263)_Use of the CPR and RPC in the malpractice arena would contravene their purpose._ _As_ Mallen and Smith point out, ethics rules protect both the public _and_ the integrity of the profession.

Exact returned source excerpt

They do so by establishing and regulating a balance among the court, attorneys, and clients.

Corpus source-specific finding

Experts may draw on ethical requirements but must connect the conduct to the legal standard without naming the rules.

Exact returned source excerpt

To avoid confusion in practice, we point out experts on an attorney's duty of care may still properly base then-opinion, as Professor Boerner did in this case, on an attorney's failure to conform to an ethics rule.

Exact returned source excerpt

In so testifying, however, the expert must address the breach of the _legal_ duty of care, and not simply the supposed breach of the ethics rules.

Corpus source-specific finding

The holding preserves existing civil remedies and other judicial uses of ethics rules.

Exact returned source excerpt

Underlying our decision not to extend the CPR and RPC into the malpractice arena is the conviction that plaintiffs already have available adequate and recognized common law theories under which to bring malpractice actions.

Exact returned source excerpt

The CPR and RPC do not enlarge upon an attorney's common law duties.

Corpus framing of this source: Hizey v. Carpenter: Separating Professional Discipline from Legal Malpractice

Corpus synthesis of this source: The Washington Supreme Court held that violations of the Code of Professional Responsibility (CPR) or Rules of Professional Conduct (RPC) neither create a malpractice cause of action nor constitute evidence of malpractice. It treated disciplinary standards and civil duties as separate regimes, barred experts and jury instructions from identifying the rules in malpractice cases, and preserved other uses of the rules and established common-law remedies.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court rejected ethics-rule violations as a basis for civil malpractice liability.

Exact returned source excerpt

Neither the CPR nor the RPC purports to set the standard for civil liability.

Exact returned source excerpt

Quite to the contrary, the preliminary statement to the RPC states: > The rules make no attempt to prescribe either disciplinary procedures or penalties for violation of a rule, nor do they undertake to define standards for civil liability of lawyers for professional conduct.

Corpus source-specific finding

Malpractice and professional discipline serve different standards and purposes.

Exact returned source excerpt

Malpractice liability, on the other hand, is premised on the conduct of the "reasonable" lawyer.

Exact returned source excerpt

One court has succinctly stated the problem created when an alleged code violation is used as evidence of a breach of the standard of care: > [I]n a civil action charging malpractice, the standard of care is the _particular duty_owed the client under the circumstances of the representation, which may or may not be the standard contemplated by the Code.
