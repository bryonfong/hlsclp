Corpus framing of this source: Indian legal outsourcing: employment gains, restricted mobility and barriers to worker organisation

Corpus synthesis of this source: Suddhabrata Deb Roy and Sreyan Chatterjee’s May 2025 workers’ inquiry draws on interviews and field research in Bengaluru and Mumbai. It interprets Indian legal process outsourcing through a Marxist account of global production and workplace control, recognising its income advantages while arguing that repetitive work, professional exclusion and weak representation undermine workers’ prospects. The authors recommend organising around salaried-worker status and building international alliances.

Corpus source-specific finding

The inquiry treats legal outsourcing as a site of cross-border workplace control, not simply a new legal-services business model.

Exact returned source excerpt

Drawing from interviews and field research in Bengaluru and Mumbai, the inquiry reveals their conditions of social, technical, and political composition, which are often rendered completely invisible within mainstream frameworks – both in India and abroad.

Exact returned source excerpt

It bases itself upon the classic workers’ inquiry method that entails investigating workplaces and employment relations from workers’ perspectives to provide a basis for organising them.

Corpus source-specific finding

India’s outsourcing advantages combine low costs with institutional, linguistic and time-zone compatibility.

Exact returned source excerpt

describe the structural reasons why the long-term core of offshore Legal Process Outsourcing (LPO) work may remain entrenched in the LPO industry in India.[6](#fn:6) First, India shares a common law system with the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and Australia (and much of the global North) because of the colonial legacy of legal systems.

Exact returned source excerpt

Second, India churns out many qualified lawyers (with varying levels of competence), establishing a steady supply of legal personnel who are available for approximately 20% of the cost of their counterparts onshore.[7] Finally, the time difference between many countries of the Global North and India syncs with available communication technologies such that legal support is offered around the clock for critical projects.[8](#fn:8) Two legal workers who have now settled in Bengaluru and Mumbai respectively after migrating from Guwahati – the capital of the northeastern state of Assam which is one of the most underdeveloped regions of the country – have narrated about their experiences while working for LPOs.

This spur in work for LPOs is not a result of businesses valuing legal work more but rather the result of a great unbundling of legal work, with companies now using the trend of smaller budgets to exercise far more control over day- to-day activities than in the earlier retainership model of external law firms or hiring of internal legal counsel.

Corpus framing of this source: Legal Workers and Legal Process Outsourcing (LPO) in India

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source presents Indian legal process outsourcing as a product of the neoliberal restructuring and unbundling of legal work. It argues that LPOs evolved from comparatively attractive corporate alternatives into workplaces marked by client control, cost pressure, labour extraction, poor work-life balance and weak worker representation. It supports this account with worker testimony, workforce figures and references to research on workplaces, legal outsourcing and paralegals, while recommending representative organisations grounded in workers’ interests rather than professional identity.

Corpus source-specific finding

The source locates Indian LPOs within a broader transformation of white-collar work and the legal sector.

Exact returned source excerpt

The basic premise within which such a mode of control is inflicted upon the society primarily stems from the kind of workplaces that are rendered mainstream in contemporary society, which are built to ensure a level of productivity and co-operation among the workers.

Exact returned source excerpt

Modern office spaces emerged in the 1960s in the Global North, but some of their most pernicious effects are being felt within the corporate sector that have emerged in the Global South.

Corpus source-specific finding

Cost, flexibility and English proficiency drive the outsourcing of paralegal work to India.

Exact returned source excerpt

India has a staggering number of 63,759 lawyers and 69,290 paralegals.[5](#fn:5) In recent times, outsourcing of paralegal work to India has become a highly lucrative option for many law firms in the Global North, especially those from the United Kingdom And the US.

Exact returned source excerpt

The reasons that many law firms in the Global North prefer Indian paralegals over and above their western counterparts are threefold: cheap wages, higher labour flexibility and better proficiency in English relative to other countries of the Global South.

Corpus source-specific finding

Early LPOs offered a comparatively attractive middle path, but their challenge to traditional law firms proved limited.

Exact returned source excerpt

When LPOs (or alternative legal services providers) were initially introduced in much of the Global South including India, they started offering better work-life balance and fair pay compared to the traditional legal employers and litigation agencies.

Exact returned source excerpt

Many of these workplaces positioned themselves as being pathways to corporate career options for lawyers who needed a middle ground between the partnership track at law firms and private practice as litigating lawyers.

Corpus source-specific finding

The author argues that contemporary LPOs face shrinking outsourced work and respond by intensifying labour extraction.

Exact returned source excerpt

Contemporary LPOs remain structurally sandwiched between these interests as they compete for an ever-decreasing slice of outsourced legal work.

Exact returned source excerpt

This means that a lot of these firms place an overarching emphasis on the extraction of relative surplus value to extend their profits, which is a natural trait of contemporary forms of capitalism, as Tronti had highlighted back in the 1960s itself.[17](#fn:17) This attitude of the law firms has led to grave consequences for many of the legal workers, often resulting in deaths due to exhaustion and organ failure.

Corpus source-specific finding

Worker testimony depicts long hours, inadequate leave and migration-related isolation.

Exact returned source excerpt

Most of our office timings are after 8 or 9 PM, no time for family, or friends.

Exact returned source excerpt

You have to forget about family and friends and community.

Corpus source-specific finding

Workers describe low-value assignments, inadequate budgets and an ambiguous professional status.

Exact returned source excerpt

These issues also come to influence the problems that workers face themselves within the organisations that they remain a part of including the organisations that are supposed to represent their interests, as the young lawyer from Guwahati now working in Mumbai referred to earlier states: > To begin with, we are lawyers, and we need to be treated as such.

Exact returned source excerpt

The work is very little, mostly coming from companies who want help with local legal issues, and that too only because they cannot find somebody in the UK or Canada.

Corpus source-specific finding

The source attributes weak LPO unionisation to professional identity, labour bureaucracy and political-party dependence.

Exact returned source excerpt

On being asked regarding the difficulties of unionisation, despite such dire conditions of legal workers, the same outsourced worker states: > We have been trying for years now.

Exact returned source excerpt

But the main problem is that when people become lawyers or legal workers, they think they have become something else entirely.

Corpus source-specific finding

The source recommends legally recognised worker representation that does not make lawyers’ professional identity paramount.
