Corpus synthesis of this source: The source raises a conditional concern that AI over-delegation could impair future clinicians’ learning, rather than asserting inevitable deskilling among experienced practitioners. It recommends preserving human reasoning and patient-facing skills, and discusses how LLM-assisted care complicates liability while clinicians remain generally regarded as the final decision-makers. The supplied excerpt offers conceptual reasoning and hypothetical risks, not empirical findings.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is crucial to note that the discussion does not assert that LLMs will definitively lead to the deskilling of *current* practitioners: these professionals have developed expertise through extensive experience and rigorous academic training, establishing a solid foundation not readily compromised by AI tools.

Exact returned source excerpt

Instead, the concern is more pronounced for the *next generation* of health care professionals — particularly medical students who might increasingly use AI for educational tasks, where over-delegating could attenuate the development of critical analytical skills and a comprehensive understanding of medical concepts traditionally cultivated through deep engagement.

If future generations of clinicians grow accustomed to AI doing the bulk of diagnostic review and analysis, their own diagnostic skills might not develop as fully; should they be required to review patient charts manually due to AI failures, they may find the task daunting.

Corpus framing of this source: Clinician trust, self-referential learning and deskilling risks from health-care LLMs

Corpus synthesis of this source: Choudhury and Chaudhry’s 2024 viewpoint develops a conceptual model linking clinician reliance on LLMs, declining human skill development and an increasing share of AI-generated training data. It predicts that these forces could reinforce one another, eventually reducing both clinician competence and model performance. The authors qualify these outcomes as anticipated rather than established, distinguish risks to future trainees from effects on experienced practitioners, and recommend assistive deployment with verification, oversight, transparency, training and shared accountability.

Corpus source-specific finding

LLMs offer practical benefits but create reliability risks in high-stakes health care.

Exact returned source excerpt

Despite ongoing challenges, generative AI like large language models (LLMs) is already being deployed in the public sphere, used by health care workers, researchers, and the public.

Exact returned source excerpt

Although LLMs have shown promise in medical assessments, scientific writing, eHealth care, and patient classification, their integration marks a paradigm shift introducing new AI complexities.

Corpus source-specific finding

The central contribution is a predicted feedback loop between AI-generated training data, clinician reliance, deskilling and declining model quality.

Exact returned source excerpt

This paper explores the evolving relationship among clinician trust in LLMs, the transition of data sources from predominantly human-generated to artificial intelligence (AI)–generated content, and the subsequent impact on the performance of LLMs and clinician competence.

Exact returned source excerpt

One of the primary concerns identified in this paper is the LLMs' self-referential learning loops, where AI-generated content feeds into the learning algorithms, threatening the diversity of the data pool, potentially entrenching biases, and reducing the efficacy of LLMs.

Corpus source-specific finding

Self-referential learning could homogenise knowledge and reinforce biases even under curated medical fine-tuning.

Exact returned source excerpt

This paradox occurs when AI-generated human-like content becomes so widespread that the AI begins to reference its own generated content, potentially leading to an echo-chamber effect where original, human-generated insights become diluted or harder to distinguish.

Exact returned source excerpt

Consequences include the propagation of biases, increased homogeneity in generated data, and ultimately hindered performance.
