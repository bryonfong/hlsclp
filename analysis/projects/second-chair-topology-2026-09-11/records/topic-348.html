Native topic findings

  1. Benchmark quality and real-world suitability are separate questions; a high score does not by itself establish reliable professional performance.
  2. The strongest direct evidence concerns benchmark design and reproducibility, not measured workplace outcomes.
  3. Several distinct mechanisms can make benchmark scores misleading.
  4. Evaluation must account for interaction, failure modes and change over time, not only isolated task success.
  5. Benchmark governance involves institutional incentives and representation as well as technical quality.
  6. The practical response is qualified, lifecycle-based assurance rather than a universal benchmark.
  7. The supplied evidence establishes methodological tensions, but not comparative verdicts on legal AI, automated judges or productivity.
