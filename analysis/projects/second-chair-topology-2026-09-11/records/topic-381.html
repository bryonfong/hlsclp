Native topic findings

  1. AI assistance does not transfer professional responsibility: lawyers must retain judgment, and judges must retain judicial authority.
  2. Verification means checking substantive support, not merely detecting nonexistent citations.
  3. The extent of verification remains task-sensitive; rigorous filing checks do not necessarily require duplicating every automated task.
  4. Specialised tools may improve research, but product labels and repeated sanctions stories do not establish reliability.
  5. Accountability increasingly reaches supervisors and institutional leaders, not just the person entering prompts.
  6. Sanctions protect clients and court processes, but culpability, mitigation and proof of AI involvement still matter.
  7. The main regulatory disagreement concerns additional safeguards, not whether humans remain responsible.
  8. Confidentiality requires tool-specific data controls; consent and enterprise branding are not complete safeguards.
  9. Judicial decision-making and witness evidence attract safeguards beyond ordinary drafting review.
  10. Verification costs and retained expertise qualify claims of efficiency and access to justice.
  11. Preserving the expertise needed to supervise AI creates a further dispute over education and adoption.
