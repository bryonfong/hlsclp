Corpus framing of this source: Testing disruptive innovation theory against its own cases

Corpus synthesis of this source: Andrew A. King and Baljir Baatartogtokh question disruptive innovation theory’s empirical foundation and generalizability. Their expert-based review reportedly finds that few of Christensen’s examples meet all four defining elements. They recommend retaining useful parts of the theory alongside other strategic approaches, rather than treating it as a universal explanation. The supplied record contains a partial article and institutional accounts of its results, not the full results section.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors distinguish Christensen’s explanation from conventional accounts of incumbent failure.

Exact returned source excerpt

In his 1997 book, The Innovator’s Dilemma, Christensen provided an explanation for the failure of respected and well-managed companies.[2](#ref2) Good managers face a dilemma, he argued, because by doing the very things they need to do to succeed — listen to customers, invest in the business, and build distinctive capabilities — they run the risk of ignoring rivals with “disruptive” innovations.[3](#ref3) Christensen’s theory of disruptive innovation has gripped the business consciousness like few other ideas.

Exact returned source excerpt

Scholars had long argued that companies are displaced when their capabilities become obsolete or surpassed by those of competitors.

Corpus source-specific finding

Widespread influence has outpaced empirical testing.

Exact returned source excerpt

Our first discovery was that, despite the theory’s widespread use and appeal, its essential validity and generalizability have been seldom tested in the academic literature.

Exact returned source excerpt

Christensen and his associates have proposed disruption as a framework for thinking about vexing social problems such as poverty, lack of access to health care, illiteracy, and unemployment.[6](#ref6) The theory, or variations thereof, has been used in so many settings that Christensen himself has expressed unease with some of the ways the theory is being applied.

Corpus source-specific finding

The study tested four defining elements using expert judgments and resolved disagreements in the theory’s favor.

Exact returned source excerpt

In it, King and co-author Baljir Baartartogtokh surveyed 79 experts on 77 examples of disruption claimed by Christensen.

Exact returned source excerpt

They asked the experts if the examples met each of the four elements of disruption that King and Baartartogtokh identified.

Corpus source-specific finding

Only 9% of the cases reportedly met all four criteria; incumbents survived or thrived in 39%.

Exact returned source excerpt

They found that only nine percent of Christensen’s 77 cases actually matched all the elements of Christensen’s own theory.

Exact returned source excerpt

(Those four qualities were: Incumbents were successful at sustaining innovation in their own target market; that innovation "overshot" the needs of a substantial number of customers; the incumbents had the capability of responding to the challenge from a newcomer; and the incumbents floundered afterward.) Fully 39 percent of the time, for instance, incumbents survived or even thrived after being disrupted, King and Baatartogtokh found.

Corpus source-specific finding

Managers should use disruption theory selectively, alongside other strategic perspectives.

Exact returned source excerpt

“The threats faced by the companies in our sample were deeply challenging, and they cannot be understood from a single viewpoint or solved by a single prescription,” King and Baartartogtokh write.

Exact returned source excerpt

In that spirit, we do not advocate discarding the theory of disruption.
