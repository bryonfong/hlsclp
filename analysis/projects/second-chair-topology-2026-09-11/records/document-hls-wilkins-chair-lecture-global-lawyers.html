Corpus framing of this source: Wilkins on global legal practice: structural change alongside continuity

Corpus synthesis of this source: Wilkins argues that long-running globalization, technological change and blurred professional boundaries are reshaping legal services. He links these developments to unbundling, output-based assessment and global competition, while stressing that most lawyers still practise in familiar forms.

Corpus source-specific finding

Structural change predates the 2008 crash, which intensified rather than initiated it.

He cautioned that while “it’s too early to tell” which of these views is correct, “it’s probably unlikely to be either.” Wilkins observed, “We’re seeing big trends that have been going on long before the crash of 2008, that were accentuated by the crash and that are likely to have important implications for everything we do.” He provided several examples of these changes, including  “the globalization of economic activity, … the rise of information technology,” and the “blurring together of the categories of knowledge that we have come to rely on at least since the 19th century,” even “the distinctions between the professions themselves,” such as law and business, for example.

Corpus source-specific finding

Changing professional boundaries and organizational forms reduce information asymmetry and enable service unbundling.

Exact returned source excerpt

According to Wilkins, the blurring of these distinctions along with changes to organizational forms, has led to a huge “reduction in information asymmetry” and to “the unbundling of services along global supply chains.” In this new world, he said, we measure less by “inputs” such as reputation and credentials, than by “value added outputs.” And in today’s world, he continued, the institutionalization of legal practice and its globalization has moved the profession “from firms that are sealed off tightly from one another to much more fluid networks,” leading to  “intense competition across global markets.” Despite these changes, “It’s important to recognize that most lawyers around the world continue to practice in ways that are very similar to the ways in which they practiced a generation and a century ago, even in the United States” through solo practice or small firm practice, he said.

Corpus synthesis of this source: The lecture report presents Wilkins’s questions about how legal education should respond to global markets and his argument that independence and commitment to the public good underpin lawyers’ distinctive legitimacy.

Exact returned source excerpt

This raises an important array of questions for legal education, Wilkins said, including whether the purpose of this education is to socialize students into this new world or challenge it.

Exact returned source excerpt

“Should we embrace the market, fight it or try to lead it?” he asked.

Corpus source-specific finding

Lawyers’ distinctive status requires a justification beyond responding to market demands.

Exact returned source excerpt

How we figure out the answers, said Wilkins, is important “not just for us but … for society” because of the role lawyers will continue to “play in the development of the modern state.” Yet he ended with a final question:  If lawyers just respond to the challenges of the global marketplace, why should they be entitled to any particular autonomy or distinction?

Exact returned source excerpt

One of the answers, he concluded, is that “clients need the legitimacy of lawyers who are themselves legitimate.” The idea that lawyers are independent and have some commitment to the public good means they bring something to the table “you can’t get from your public relations specialist.” *– Greg DiBella*
