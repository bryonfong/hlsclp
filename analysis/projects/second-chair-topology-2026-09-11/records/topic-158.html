Native topic record

Credence incentives: More information may not improve service

Corpus synthesis: Combines theory, experiments and field evidence to show quality depends on incentives, liability and institutional design.

This record retains the provider’s full available topic findings and source perspectives. It does not claim a new independent analytical review. Positions refer to this topic’s proposition: “Redirects” is not simple opposition, and “Unclear” is not support.

Declared lineage: Cross-domain institutional evidence: Innovation and market correction remain conditional on incentives, gatekeeping and measurement · Credence services: Verifiability alone cannot secure appropriate treatment

4 distinct stance-bearing works · Builds on: 4

Native topic findings

  1. Credence-goods economics frames expert-service quality as an incentive and accountability problem, not simply a shortage of consumer information.
  2. Price incentives can eliminate fraud in theory, but only with specific supporting institutions and assumptions.
  3. Controlled experimental evidence challenges the theoretical equivalence of liability and verifiability.
  4. More information, disclosure and second opinions do not reliably improve outcomes; information design changes incentives.
  5. Field evidence supports incentive-sensitive provision, while diagnostic uncertainty and social motives complicate simple profit-maximising accounts.
  6. Practical safeguards should target the particular failure and account for the costs of changing service organisation.

Academia & research · Automation debate (2015-2022)

Corpus framing of this source: Credence goods: expert fraud, conditional remedies and institutional design

Corpus synthesis of this source: Balafoutas and Kerschbamer’s 2020 review synthesises theory, laboratory experiments and field evidence on fraud in expert-service markets. They distinguish unsuitable provision from fraudulent billing and show why information, incentives and institutional safeguards must be considered together. Their contribution is a qualified synthesis of existing research, not a new empirical study or a universal prescription.

Academia & research · Foundations (pre-2015)

Corpus framing of this source: Liability, competition and expert incentives in credence-goods markets

Corpus synthesis of this source: Dulleck, Kerschbamer and Sutter report a 936-participant experiment testing liability, verifiability, reputation and competition. They find that liability improves efficiency much more than verifiability, while competition expands trade without necessarily improving treatment. They also test seller motives beyond profit maximisation and identify unresolved behavioural patterns.
