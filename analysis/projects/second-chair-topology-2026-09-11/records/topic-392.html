Native topic findings

  1. The central proposition is that AI can assist legal work, but it cannot assume responsibility for its accuracy.
  2. The debate extends beyond invented cases to misleading uses of genuine authorities, and citation accuracy remains distinct from argument quality.
  3. Empirical research supports persistent, task-dependent errors, not a single legal-AI error rate or an assured improvement trajectory.
  4. Court incidents establish concrete consequences, but neither incident counts nor repeated reporting measure overall AI reliability.
  5. Sanctions depend on the applicable authority and circumstances; candour and correction matter alongside the initial error.
  6. Responsibility extends through supervision and supplied material, making operational review more important than an AI policy alone.
  7. The principal regulatory disagreement concerns AI-specific disclosure and restrictions, not whether submissions must be accurate.
  8. Automated checking can support human verification, but its limitations shift attention to legal-data access and transparent testing.
  9. Equal verification duties create an access-to-justice tension when litigants lack the expertise or resources to fulfil them.
  10. Faster production can increase institutional checking burdens even as individual outputs improve, while accuracy safeguards leave other risks unresolved.
