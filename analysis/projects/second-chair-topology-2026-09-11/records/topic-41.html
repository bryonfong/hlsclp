Corpus source-specific finding

The court assigns Snell the burden of establishing coverage because the specified-operations provision defines coverage rather than excludes it.

Exact returned source excerpt

The policy states simply that “[the] Insured performs landscaping.” In short, the Specified Operations provision (fitting into the gap left by the general coverage provision) describes the contours or boundaries of coverage—it does not purport to take away coverage already granted.

Corpus source-specific finding

Snell’s insurance application defeats coverage without requiring a definitive interpretation of landscaping.

Exact returned source excerpt

The court noted that the policy did not define the word “landscaping,” and it concluded that the “common, everyday meaning of the word” did not include trampoline installation “even [under] the definitions submitted by Snell” in his briefing.

Exact returned source excerpt

Even taking the term “landscaping” as ambiguous, construing it in Snell’s favor, and applying Alabama law’s broad understanding of the causal term “arises out of,” Snell’s claim still fails.

Corpus source-specific finding

Alabama law permits an indemnity ruling before the underlying action reaches judgment in some circumstances.

Exact returned source excerpt

In sum, we hold that the duty to indemnify under Alabama insurance law can, at least under some circumstances, be decided before the underlying action reaches judgment.

Corpus source-specific finding

Snell’s bad-faith claim fails both because United lawfully denied coverage and because his investigation complaints do not meet the required standard.

Exact returned source excerpt

Snell advances what the Alabama Supreme Court calls an “abnormal” bad faith claim—which is focused on bad faith failure to investigate.

Exact returned source excerpt

A plaintiff advancing an “abnormal” bad faith claim must prove that the insurer (1) intentionally or recklessly failed to investigate a claim; (2) intentionally or recklessly failed to properly subject a claim to a cognitive evaluation or review; (3) manufactured a debatable reason to deny a claim; or (4) relied on an ambiguous portion of a policy as a lawful basis for denying a claim.
