Corpus framing of this source: Entry-level automation can weaken growth by changing who mentors novices

Corpus synthesis of this source: Automation, AI, and the Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge presents a theoretical mechanism linking entry-level automation to weaker intergenerational learning. Under its stated assumptions, automation raises output immediately but slows growth permanently and eventually leaves output below the no-shock path. The supplied passage establishes model results, not empirical findings or policy recommendations.

Corpus source-specific finding

The analysis considers an automation shock that preserves novice employment and positive growth.

Exact returned source excerpt

### 5.1 Improvements in Entry-Level Automation Suppose an unanticipated technological shock at date τ allows an additional measure ∆ of tasks to be automated, with N − I − ∆ > 1 (so the post-shock economy continues to grow and all novices remain employed).

Exact returned source excerpt

I focus on two specific advances: improvements in entry-level automation and the creation of new tasks.

Corpus source-specific finding

Automation produces immediate output gains but eventually lowers output relative to the no-shock path.

Exact returned source excerpt

The ratio of post-shock to no-shock output decomposes into immediate adoption gains (intensive margin: machines outperform novices, (m/h)^∆; extensive margin: displaced novices reallocate toward less-skilled experts, enabling a new tier to produce) and long-run losses.

Exact returned source excerpt

**Proposition 4 (Output Effects of an Automation Shock).** Relative to the no-shock scenario: (1) **Immediate Adoption Gains** — output is strictly higher on impact; (2) **Permanent Growth Slowdown** — the growth rate of output is strictly lower for all t ≥ τ; (3) **Long-Run Reversal** — there exists a date t* such that output is strictly lower for all t > t*.

Corpus source-specific finding

Weaker mentor composition creates a compounding loss of tacit knowledge.

Exact returned source excerpt

The long-run losses are the dynamic counterpart of the equilibrium reallocation of labor: as highly skilled experts hire fewer novices, displaced novices are reassigned to less-skilled experts, acquire lower-quality tacit knowledge, and become worse mentors themselves.

Exact returned source excerpt

The best practices of top experts thus diffuse more slowly, imposing a compounding drag on future output.

Corpus source-specific finding

Reduced hands-on practice is plausible but unnecessary for the model's growth loss.

Exact returned source excerpt

A common intuition is that improvements in automation harm skill accumulation by depriving novices of hands-on practice.

Exact returned source excerpt

While that mechanism is plausible, **Proposition 4 shows it is not necessary: a deterioration in mentor composition alone is sufficient to impair growth.

Corpus framing of this source: Automation, AI, and the Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge

Corpus synthesis of this source: The paper models expertise as embodied tacit knowledge that requires direct expert involvement to transmit. It argues that technology’s long-run effects depend chiefly on how it reallocates novices among experts: automation may displace learning opportunities or let experts supervise at greater scale, whereas task-creating technology expands novice access to top experts and produces immediate output, growth and welfare gains in the model. Extensions qualify these results by task learning value, supervision constraints and transmission quality.

Corpus source-specific finding

Expertise consists of embodied tacit knowledge that complements codified procedures and requires direct expert involvement to transmit.

Exact returned source excerpt

Tacit knowledge, in contrast, encompasses intuitive skills and insights that are difficult to articulate precisely (Polanyi, 1966; Foray, 2004).

Exact returned source excerpt

These two forms of knowledge are interdependent: codified instructions usually require tacit knowledge to be properly interpreted and adapted to real-world complexities.

Corpus source-specific finding

Technology affects knowledge transmission primarily by reallocating novices among experts, rather than through its technological category alone.

Exact returned source excerpt

**The Reallocation Margin.—** What matters for knowledge transmission is not the technological category per se, but how the technology reshapes the assignment of novices across experts.

Exact returned source excerpt

Technologies that move novices away from leading experts weaken the diffusion of best practices; technologies that expand novices' access to those experts strengthen it.

Corpus source-specific finding

Task-creating technology produces immediate output, sustained growth and welfare gains in the model by inducing top experts to hire and train more novices.

Exact returned source excerpt

**Proposition 6 (Output and Welfare Effects of a Task-Creating Technology).** Relative to the no-shock scenario: (1) **Immediate Adoption Gains** — output strictly higher on impact; (2) **Growth Acceleration** — growth rate strictly higher for all t ≥ τ; (3) **Welfare Gains** — the shock is strictly welfare-increasing.

Exact returned source excerpt

Unlike improvements in automation, this technology generates both immediate and long-run gains, by prompting the highest-skilled experts to hire additional novices and thereby accelerating the diffusion of best practices.

Corpus source-specific finding

Once experts can scale across projects, automation has an ambiguous long-run effect because novice displacement competes with expanded expert span of control.

Exact returned source excerpt

Once experts can scale, the growth rate depends on the total number of novices active experts supervise across projects, not only the number required within each project.

Exact returned source excerpt

For an automation shock, the effect on span of control (and thus long-run growth) is now ambiguous, balancing a *displacement effect* (allocating tasks to machines reduces novices per project) against a *scale effect* (machines require less supervision, allowing experts to oversee more concurrent projects).

Corpus source-specific finding

The paper characterizes displacement as more salient than expert scaling in generative AI evidence reviewed so far, while treating that conclusion as contingent rather than universal.
