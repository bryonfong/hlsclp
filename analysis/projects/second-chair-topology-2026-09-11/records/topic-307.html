Native topic record

Legal judgment: Correct output may remain insufficient

Corpus synthesis: Argues automation increases contextual judgment and verification duties while questioning whether assisted performance develops supervisory expertise.

This record retains the provider’s full available topic findings and source perspectives. It does not claim a new independent analytical review. Positions refer to this topic’s proposition: “Redirects” is not simple opposition, and “Unclear” is not support.

Declared lineage: Legal-service market restructuring: Bounded AI gains become structural change through institutional redesign · Legal-service restructuring: Bounded automation redistributes work, value and risk

7 distinct stance-bearing works · Builds on: 3 · Mixed: 1 · Supports: 3

Native topic findings

  1. The central proposition is that automating legal outputs can increase the importance of contextual judgement without preserving every lawyer task.
  2. Independent judgement and verification impose responsibilities that apparently competent AI output does not discharge.
  3. The main disagreement concerns whether sound outputs suffice, or whether legal judgement requires something beyond output quality.
  4. Judgement’s centrality does not establish its uniqueness, infallibility or objective measurability.
  5. Better assisted performance does not necessarily develop the expertise needed to supervise AI.
  6. The proposed response combines experiential learning, reflective supervision and technical literacy.
  7. A judgement premium remains a conditional economic prediction, not a guarantee of higher fees or access to advice.

Corpus source-specific finding

Good judgement integrates legal expertise with context, experience and emotional intelligence.

Exact returned source excerpt

Ethical judgement is multi- faceted (a combination of expertise, experience, ethics and emotional intelligence), practical (the application of the law to achieve some end or solve a problem) and wholistic (an awareness and understanding of the relevance of surrounding issues such as risks, constraints and opportunities, including legality and morality).

Exact returned source excerpt

Judgement is often required when there is uncertainty and partial information.28 It means confronting a world where ‘inexactitude is hidden [and] wildness lies in wait’.29 Judgement is multidimensional, and involves combining a range of inputs, often both legal and non-legal.30 It is a process of bringing coherence to conflicting values and sensitivity to highly contextualised facts and circumstances.31 In seeking to identify the core traits of the effective lawyer, Anthony Kronman focussed on practical wisdom.32 Kronman draws on the common law tradition and the need to identify and apply law to relevant facts to resolve disputes, or when applied to the lawyer, ‘a subtle and discriminating sense of how the (often conflicting) general­ ities of legal doctrine should be applied in concrete disputes’.33 There are choices and dis­ cretion at play, but they are guided by tradition and method.34 Judgement is the ability to identify and evaluate available choices or alternatives.

Corpus source-specific finding

Human judgement itself needs safeguards against bias, without abandoning necessary intuition.

Exact returned source excerpt

Randall Kiser is his book Soft Skills for the Effective Lawyer explains that good judge­ ment requires that the lawyer is aware of and takes steps to avoid decision making biases and heuristics such as over-confidence and confirmation bias.46 Kiser’s concern with avoiding decision making biases brings to mind Daniel Kahneman’s distinction between system 1 and system 2 modes of ‘thinking fast and slow’.47 System 1 is thinking fast with intuition and heuristics, while system 2 is slow, analytic, data-based and comprehen­ sive.48 System 1 can be prone to various biases.49 System 2 is typically more reliable, but because it requires effort and attention can be overloaded.50 Much of the discussion above may be seen as focussed on, or promoting, effective system 2 thinking.

Exact returned source excerpt

However, thinking fast, or intuitive thinking, is an important part of lawyers’ judgement.51 This is because some decisions must be made in real-time or immediately.

Exact returned source excerpt

The ethical and professional obligations of lawyers to promote the public interest58 is what sets the legal profession apart from businesses and commercial interests – ‘the world of com­ merce has different imperatives’.59 Similarly the lawyer’s ethical obligations are a central difference with AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

The lawyer owes duties to the administration of justice, the client and to their own personal independence and integrity, which govern and limit judgement.
