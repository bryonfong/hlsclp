Corpus framing of this source: United States v. Cohen — Opinion and Order Declining Sanctions over Google Bard-Fabricated Citations

Corpus synthesis of this source: Judge Jesse M. Furman denied Michael Cohen’s fourth request for early termination of supervised release and declined to sanction Cohen or his lawyer for citing three non-existent cases generated through Google Bard. The court distinguished negligent failure to verify citations from the subjective bad faith required for sanctions, while stressing counsel’s responsibility to check authorities before filing.

Corpus source-specific finding

The court denied early termination of Cohen’s supervised release because his October 2023 testimony undermined, rather than demonstrated, the claimed remorse and commitment to law.

Exact returned source excerpt

Since that time, Cohen has applied multiple times for a reduction of his sentence, each time without success.

Exact returned source excerpt

To the contrary, it is only “occasionally” justified due to “changed circumstances,” such as “exceptionally good behavior by the defendant,” that “render a previously imposed term or condition of release either too harsh or inappropriately tailored to serve the general punishment goals of section 3553(a).” Lussier, 104 F.3d at 36.

Corpus source-specific finding

Although citing non-existent cases can support sanctions, the available grounds required bad faith in this proceeding.

Exact returned source excerpt

In its Order to Show Cause, the Court cited three potential grounds for sanctions: Rule 11(b)(2), Section 1927, and the Court’s inherent power.

Exact returned source excerpt

There is no doubt that sanctions can be imposed on at least some of these grounds for the citation to non-existent cases.
