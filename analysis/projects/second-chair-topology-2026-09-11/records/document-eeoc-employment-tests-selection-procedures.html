Corpus framing of this source: Employment Tests and Selection Procedures

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source explains Title VII’s treatment of discriminatory employment tests, outlining disparate-treatment and disparate-impact analysis and illustrating the framework through cognitive- and strength-testing cases involving Ford and Dial.

Corpus source-specific finding

Title VII reaches both intentional differential treatment and facially neutral selection procedures with discriminatory effects.

Exact returned source excerpt

- Does the evidence show that the employer’s reason for the difference in treatment is untrue, and that the real reason for the different treatment is race, color, religion, sex, or national origin?

Exact returned source excerpt

- Title VII also prohibits employers from using neutral tests or selection procedures that have the **effect** of disproportionately excluding persons based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin, where the tests or selection procedures are not “job-related and consistent with business necessity.” This is called **“disparate impact”** discrimination.

Corpus source-specific finding

Disparate-impact analysis first asks whether a specific employment practice disproportionately excludes a protected group, ordinarily using statistical evidence.

Exact returned source excerpt

- Does the employer use a particular employment practice that has a **disparate impact** on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin?

Exact returned source excerpt

For example, if an employer requires that all applicants pass a physical agility test, does the test disproportionately screen out women?

Corpus source-specific finding

When a selection procedure has a disparate impact, the employer must establish that it is job-related and consistent with business necessity.

Exact returned source excerpt

- If the selection procedure has a disparate impact based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin, can the employer show that the selection **procedure is job-related and consistent with business necessity**?

Exact returned source excerpt

An employer can meet this standard by showing that it is necessary to the safe and efficient performance of the job.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Ford matter illustrates that prior validation does not by itself resolve continuing disparate impact when less discriminatory alternatives can serve the employer’s needs.

The ATSS was a written cognitive test that measured verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning in order to evaluate mechanical aptitude.

Corpus source-specific finding

Ford’s settlement required a replacement procedure designed to predict job success while reducing adverse impact and included substantial monetary relief.

Exact returned source excerpt

In the settlement agreement, Ford agreed to replace the ATSS with a selection procedure, to be designed by a jointly-selected industrial psychologist, that would predict job success and reduce adverse impact.

Exact returned source excerpt

Additionally, Ford paid \$8.55 million in monetary relief.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Dial matter supplies quantitative evidence of a strength test’s adverse impact on women while separately reporting the employer’s safety and job-similarity defense.

Exact returned source excerpt

Dial Corp.*, women were disproportionately rejected for entry-level production jobs because of a strength test.

Exact returned source excerpt

The test had a significant adverse impact on women – prior to the use of the test, 46% of hires were women; after use of the test, only 15% of hires were women.
