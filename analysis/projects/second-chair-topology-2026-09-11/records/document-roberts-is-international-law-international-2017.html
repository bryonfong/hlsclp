Corpus framing of this source: Is International Law International? — difference, dominance and disruption in the international legal field

Corpus synthesis of this source: Drawing on the Preface and Chapter 1 of Anthea Roberts’s 2017 monograph, the source develops comparative international law as a sociological account of how national and regional influences shape ostensibly universal international law. It identifies distinct professional communities, unequal transnational influence—especially Western and Anglo-American dominance—and prospective disruption from technology, domestic politics and geopolitical multipolarity. It illustrates the argument through language patterns and disputes involving Crimea, the South China Sea, sovereignty, intervention, cyberspace and trade, while qualifying broad Western/non-Western categories and recommending wider sources, networks and engagement with unlike-minded perspectives. The supplied text is partial and does not establish what later chapters say.

Disruption Examining these observations of difference and dominance together, a comparative international law approach encourages international lawyers to think about the ways that international law, and their understandings of it, may be disrupted by a variety of factors, including technological innovation, changes in domestic political preferences, and shifts in geopolitical power.
