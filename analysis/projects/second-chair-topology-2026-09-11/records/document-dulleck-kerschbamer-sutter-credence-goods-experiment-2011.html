Corpus framing of this source: Liability, competition and expert incentives in credence-goods markets

Corpus synthesis of this source: Dulleck, Kerschbamer and Sutter report a 936-participant experiment testing liability, verifiability, reputation and competition. They find that liability improves efficiency much more than verifiability, while competition expands trade without necessarily improving treatment. They also test seller motives beyond profit maximisation and identify unresolved behavioural patterns.

Corpus framing of this source: Liability, incentives and the limits of reputation in credence-goods markets

Corpus synthesis of this source: Dulleck, Kerschbamer and Sutter use a 936-participant laboratory experiment to distinguish institutions that increase trade from those that improve efficiency. They find strong benefits from requiring sufficient treatment, limited effects from making delivered treatment verifiable, and price-driven rather than quality-driven benefits from competition. Their behavioural model gives moral motives an explanatory role, while leaving several pricing and treatment patterns unresolved.

Corpus framing of this source: Credence-goods institutions, seller morality and market performance

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source develops standard self-interest and non-standard preference models for credence-goods markets, then tests liability, verifiability, reputation and competition in a 2×2×2×2 experiment. Its central empirical contribution is that liability strongly improves trade and efficiency, verifiability has little effect, competition lowers prices and expands trade without reliably improving quality, and reputation mainly induces trust when safeguards are absent. Seller honesty and price-sensitive behaviour explain some departures from standard theory, but the non-standard model remains incomplete.
