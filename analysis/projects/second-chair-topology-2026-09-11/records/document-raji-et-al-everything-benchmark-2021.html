Open topic: Evaluation validity: Benchmark safeguards impose trade-offs

Corpus framing of this source: Contextualised evaluation rather than claims of benchmark generality

Corpus synthesis of this source: AI and the Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark challenges the construct validity of using dataset scores to establish context-independent capabilities. The authors retain a role for tightly scoped benchmarks, warn that research incentives and marketing inflate their significance, and recommend broader evaluation methods to understand model behaviour and limitations.

Open topic: Work redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Corpus framing of this source: AI and the Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark

Corpus synthesis of this source: The authors argue that finite, contextual datasets cannot meaningfully measure general machine intelligence. Using ImageNet, GLUE and SuperGLUE, they show how arbitrary task selection, embedded perspectives, aggregate metrics and competitive incentives can turn scoped tests into misleading proxies for broad capability and deployment readiness. They recommend tightly scoped benchmarks supplemented by diagnostic evaluation methods that expose failures, disparities, component contributions and practical system properties.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors reject claims that finite benchmarks measure general capability.

Exact returned source excerpt

In particular, we argue that benchmarks presented as measurements of progress towards general ability within vague tasks such as “visual understanding” or “language understanding” are as ineffective as the finite museum is at representing “everything in the whole wide world,” and for similar reasons — being inherently specific, finite and contextual.

Exact returned source excerpt

Benchmarks like GLUE [Wang et al., 2019a] or ImageNet [Deng et al., 2009] are often elevated to become definitions of the essential common tasks to validate the performance of any given model.

Corpus source-specific finding

Purportedly general benchmarks often assemble convenient or arbitrary tasks rather than theory-grounded representations of broad abilities.

Exact returned source excerpt

If the so-called “general” benchmarks were legitimate tests of progress towards general artificial cognitive abilities, we would expect the tasks they embody to be chosen systematically, or with reference to specific theories of the cognitive abilities they model.

Exact returned source excerpt

Instead, what we observe looks more like samples of convenience: tasks and collections of tasks arbitrarily built out of what is easily available to the team developing these benchmarks, even if such constructions are theoretically unsound.
