Builds onBig Four: Integration matters more than replacement

Open topic: Big Four: Integration matters more than replacement

Corpus framing of this source: Eversheds’ multidisciplinary response to Big Four competition

Corpus synthesis of this source: “Navigating a Brave New World” (January/February 2016) uses Eversheds to examine how traditional law firms combine legal and consulting services in response to client demands and Big Four competition. It draws on recruitment examples and firm leaders’ accounts, while leaving client preferences and future ownership structures unresolved.

Corpus source-specific finding

Client demands and wider market changes push law firms toward integrated services.

Exact returned source excerpt

Increasingly sophisticated clients are demanding much more from their law firms for much less, including problem solving that straddles across disciplinary lines, such as law and business, technology, and strategy (see “[A Global Age of More for Less](https://thepractice.law.harvard.edu/issue/volume-1-issue-1/)”).

Exact returned source excerpt

Regulatory changes have also opened the door to alternative business structures and unconventional modes of operation (see “[Disruptive Innovation in the Market for Legal Services](https://thepractice.law.harvard.edu/issue/volume-1-issue-2/)”).

Corpus source-specific finding

Eversheds illustrates diversification within a traditional law firm.

Exact returned source excerpt

It offers a traditional set of legal services, ranging from M&A work to real estate transactions to IP litigation to corporate law.

Exact returned source excerpt

To diversify their services and increase top-line revenues, as well as to respond to clients demanding more-integrated problem solving, innovative firms like Eversheds are leading the way toward combining deep legal expertise with nonlegal offerings.

Corpus source-specific finding

Recruitment from the Big Four provides concrete evidence of competition for talent.

Exact returned source excerpt

As a case in point, Eversheds Consulting recently hired away a former Ernst & Young partner and a former KPMG partner to head up its new financial services regulatory compliance team—a move that generated significant attention in the legal press.

Exact returned source excerpt

Paul Worth, the head of Eversheds’ financial institution group, explains in a statement announcing the hires: The true differentiator here is that we will have lawyers and consultants under the same roof.

Corpus source-specific finding

Multidisciplinary services require both outside expertise and deliberate internal integration.

Exact returned source excerpt

Second, the firm actively recruits and employs nonlawyer professionals to sit alongside and work with lawyers.

Exact returned source excerpt

Although he is not a trained lawyer, having come to Eversheds from IT consultancy work, he has deep experience in technology and how to integrate it with company goals.

Corpus source-specific finding

Operational integration does not necessarily confer partnership or ownership.

Exact returned source excerpt

It should be made clear that while nonlawyer consultants at Eversheds are internally considered on the same level as traditional law partners, they do not carry the partner title or have equity in the firm (likely due to regulatory restrictions).

Exact returned source excerpt

As of this writing, neither Eversheds nor any of the United Kingdom’s Magic Circle firms have thus far applied for ABS.

Corpus source-specific finding

The article leaves competitive outcomes open but stresses adjacent capabilities and talent.

Exact returned source excerpt

It is an open question as to whether clients will prefer the integrated solutions offered by the Big Four or those increasingly being offered by innovative firms such as Eversheds.

Exact returned source excerpt

What this move does seem to reflect is that both sides recognize that insofar as clients are seeking integrated solutions, there is an urgent need to incorporate and integrate more adjacent services and functions to their core businesses.

Open topic: Legal economics: AI makes adoption a model problem

Corpus framing of this source: Law firms diversify into advisory services, but integration remains uncertain

Corpus synthesis of this source: Navigating a Brave New World cites law firms’ expansion into nonlegal advisory services, predicts further entrants and questions how deeply firms can integrate these offerings. It argues that future success may depend on adaptation and responsiveness to clients.

Corpus source-specific finding

Law firms have begun expanding into adjacent consulting and corporate advisory services.

Exact returned source excerpt

For instance, in 2014, Mayer Brown launched a consulting arm in Asia, hiring an international trade advisory team.

Exact returned source excerpt

Similarly, in 2015, DLA Piper announced the formation of a new corporate advisory arm, dubbed “Noble Street,” which will focus on financing, corporate, and M&A activities within the media, entertainment, technology, and sports industries.

Corpus source-specific finding

The source expects other firms to follow, while qualifying the prospects for deep integration.

Exact returned source excerpt

Other firms will most likely follow in their footsteps.

Exact returned source excerpt

Through all these initiatives, however, critical questions remain, including the extent to which law firms will be able to deeply integrate nonlegal products into their traditional business cores, whether due to regulations or internal cultural norms.

Corpus source-specific finding

Adaptation and responsiveness to clients may shape firms’ future success or failure.

Exact returned source excerpt

Nevertheless, it is clear that change has come to the legal services market, and the successes or failures of law firms going forward may well be defined by how well they grow and adapt—and by how well they can respond to client needs and demands.
