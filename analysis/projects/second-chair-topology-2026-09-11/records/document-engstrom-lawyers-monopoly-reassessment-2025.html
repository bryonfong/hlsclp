Corpus synthesis of this source: David Freeman Engstrom and Nora Freeman Engstrom’s 2025 introduction argues that unmet civil legal need and emerging legal technology make regulatory change necessary and increasingly inevitable. Corpus source-specific finding

The editors argue that the justice gap and legal technology are destabilising lawyers’ longstanding monopoly.

Exact returned source excerpt

Sweeping unauthorized practice of law (UPL) laws have prohibited (and in some cases, criminalized) the practice of law by anyone other than a licensed attorney – and these rules have further mandated that lawyers work as solo practitioners or in lawyer-owned law firms.[Footnote 1](#fn1) This one-two punch has meant that only lawyers can provide legal advice and that even lawyers can’t practice law in nonlawyer-owned entities.[Footnote 2](#fn2) Yet, that monopoly is increasingly under threat, as more and more stakeholders have begun to question lawyers’ stranglehold over the delivery of legal services.[Footnote 3](#fn3) Is it really the case that only* a licensed attorney possesses the expertise necessary to advise an individual on *every* aspect of a case?

Exact returned source excerpt

There are a few exceptions to the latter rule, including if the lawyer is working for the government or a nonprofit or is offering legal services to the entity itself (i.e., an in-house lawyer).

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

Arizona and Utah have led the charge, rewriting the rules around UPL and law practice ownership.[Footnote 4](#fn4) Other states have revised their regulatory frameworks to permit nonlawyer community justice workers to offer legal advice in at least certain circumstances.[Footnote 5](#fn5) Still others, including Colorado and Oregon, have changed their rules to permit licensed legal paraprofessionals to provide legal help.[Footnote 6](#fn6) And numerous other states, including Michigan, North Carolina, and Texas, are weighing whether to follow suit.[Footnote 7](#fn7) Put simply, after nearly a century of relative stasis, policymakers, legislatures, and lawyers are devoting serious attention to a dizzying array of reforms to liberalize – and, indeed, to rethink and remake – the legal services marketplace.[Footnote 8](#fn8) Such a convulsive change to a billion-dollar legal services industry is hardly uncontroversial, and many are on record opposing these reforms.
