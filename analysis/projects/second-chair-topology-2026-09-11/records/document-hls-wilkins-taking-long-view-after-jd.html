Corpus framing of this source: Taking the Long View: persistent career inequalities despite professional change

Corpus synthesis of this source: Elaine McArdle’s 2024 Harvard Law School article reports findings from After the JD and David Wilkins’s interpretation of them. Its central contribution is longitudinal evidence that educational and demographic advantages persist, alongside a warning that disruption need not transform professional hierarchies.

Corpus source-specific finding

The findings draw on a large, mixed-method longitudinal study rather than a snapshot of the profession.

Exact returned source excerpt

This and many other myth-busting conclusions are the result of “After the JD,” a quantitative and qualitative analysis that includes more than 5,000 law graduates who entered the bar in 2000.

Exact returned source excerpt

Professor David Wilkins, faculty director of the Harvard Law School Center on the Legal Profession Credit: Lorin Granger Launched in 2000 by a research team that included David Wilkins ’80, Lester Kissel Professor and faculty director of the Harvard Law School [Center on the Legal Profession](https://clp.law.harvard.edu/), the project tracked the careers of graduates from nearly every law school in the country, including unaccredited schools.

Corpus source-specific finding

Elite law-school advantages increase rather than fade over lawyers’ careers.

Exact returned source excerpt

But on the contrary, the benefits of going to a top-ranked law school actually increase over the course of a lawyer’s career in terms of income, opportunities for prestigious jobs, and the ability to repay law school debt, according to the findings of a 20-year longitudinal study of the careers of lawyers.

# Taking the Long View *Elaine McArdle — Harvard Law School (2024)* Taking the Long View - Harvard Law School | Harvard Law School Credit: Adam McCauley # Taking the Long View David Wilkins, part of the core research team that launched a longitudinal study on lawyers’ lives,  describes what’s changed and — despite best intentions — what hasn’t in the past 20 years Sep 30, 2024 By [Elaine McArdle](https://hls.harvard.edu/authors/elaine_mcardle/) People often assume that the advantages of attending an elite law school evaporate over the course of a lawyer’s career, because the quality of the work lawyers produce becomes more important than academic credentials.

Here, Wilkins touches on a few key takeaways: **What are some of the surprising findings about the profession today versus 20 years ago, when the study started?** While there have been some tremendous changes, there is also remarkable continuity when we look at many of the structures beneath the surface of how the bar and the legal profession are organized, how careers operate, and who tends to end up in the most prestigious, high-paying positions.

Corpus source-specific finding

Wilkins wants the evidence to inform career choices, legal education and continued research.

Exact returned source excerpt

Wilkins says the authors hope the abundance of “rich data” the book offers will be useful to three groups in particular: law students as they consider what they want from their careers; law schools as they design curricula to best serve students; and the public, because, he says, “What the profession looks like, who has access to it, and what the structures of opportunity are, are matters of interest and importance and concern far beyond the legal profession to our society as a whole.” “I’m glad I did it, but if I’d had any idea how long it would take me, I’m not sure I would have entered into this project,” Wilkins adds, with a chuckle.

I’m hoping that a young and energetic team of socio-legal researchers will decide to study how the interplay of these forces shape lawyers’ careers over the next 20 years!
