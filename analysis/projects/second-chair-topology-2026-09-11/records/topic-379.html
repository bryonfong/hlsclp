Native topic findings

  1. The cluster frames legal AI primarily as a copilot: it may assist information-intensive work, but humans retain judgment, supervision and accountability.
  2. Measured benefits are task-specific and stronger for speed and cost than for consistently improved legal quality.
  3. Evaluation must distinguish information processing from judgment-intensive legal work.
  4. Probabilistic generation creates a substantive verification problem that grounding and legal databases reduce but do not remove.
  5. Effective verification requires qualified, independent review, but its cost and feasibility remain disputed.
  6. Courts and regulators agree on human responsibility but diverge over disclosure, certification and prohibited uses.
  7. Accuracy controls alone are insufficient because confidentiality, bias, evidence integrity, billing and governance create distinct risks.
  8. Access-to-justice claims present a trade-off between broader assistance and users’ limited ability to verify legal output.
  9. The practical implication is selective, risk-based adoption rather than uniform embrace or avoidance.
