Corpus framing of this source: Accommodating Machine Learning Algorithms in Professional Service Firms

Corpus synthesis of this source: The source develops a task-level, comparative account of how English accounting and law firms accommodate machine-learning algorithms. It finds unexpectedly similar changes and professional responses across the two fields despite differences in their data and work, while limiting its direct evidence to extractive machine learning used by early adopters between 2018 and 2020. It treats generative AI’s effects as unresolved and recommends research into professional resistance, implementation outcomes and the evolving role of technologists.

Corpus framing of this source: Professional control and the cooperative accommodation of machine learning

Corpus synthesis of this source: Faulconbridge, Sarwar and Spring explain how accounting and law firms accommodate machine learning by justifying its contributions, addressing its limitations and allowing professionals to shape changes to their work. Their empirical study focuses on England; the supplied excerpts establish the central argument and research context but provide no detailed case findings.

Corpus source-specific finding

Accommodation combines a justification for adoption with responses to algorithmic limitations.

Exact returned source excerpt

Accommodating machine learning algorithms involves strategic responses that both justify adoption in the context of the possibilities and new contributions of machine learning algorithms and respond to the algorithms’ limitations and opaque (Kellogg et al., 2020) and inscrutable (Anthony, Bechky, & Fayard, 2023) nature.

Exact returned source excerpt

Accommodating machine learning algorithms is comprised of recursive processes enabled by the way professionals themselves control both how machine learning algorithms are incorporated into work and how work organization evolves in PSFs.

The evidence comprises interviews and media documents collected in 2018–2020.

Exact returned source excerpt

To address this question, this paper examines how machine learning algorithms were introduced in accounting and law firms in England.

Exact returned source excerpt

We draw on an original dataset collected between 2018 and 2020 comprising over 800 documents from the media and 80 interviews.

Corpus source-specific finding

Machine learning creates a distinctive adoption problem by changing judgement work while obscuring how determinations arise.

Exact returned source excerpt

Machine learning algorithms can automate and replace the judgement and interpretation work that professionals associate with their role and which PSFs have typically protected during digital dis- ruptions (Hinings, Gegenhuber, & Greenwood, 2018).

Exact returned source excerpt

Machine learning algorithms might, therefore, be perceived as undermining the autonomy of professionals over decisions about how work is completed and their ability to explain the decision-making that informs professional advice (Björkdahl & Kronblad, 2021; Pachidi et al., 2021).

Corpus source-specific finding

Task differentiation and cross-occupational collaboration supply possible routes to acceptable change.

Exact returned source excerpt

Studies have also shown that a potential response to disruptions to work, by machine learning technologies and other forces, is to create new task structures within PSFs that allow the structural differentiation of work (Anthony, 2021; Wilmers, 2020).

Exact returned source excerpt

This response means that professionals do not need to directly use new technologies, or the impacts are limited to small groups, often of more junior professionals.

Corpus source-specific finding

The authors leave long-term occupational power shifts open rather than predict a single outcome.

Exact returned source excerpt

Most recently, debates have focused on the way intelligent algorithmic technologies create the potential for, at one extreme, the end of the professions and professional service firms (PSFs) (Kokina & Davenport, 2017; Susskind & Susskind, 2015), or alternatively new business models for PSFs and new types of work for professionals as the services offered to clients are revolutionised (Armour & Sako, 2020; Faulconbridge, Sarwar, & Spring, 2023; Kronblad, 2020; Pemer & Werr, 2023; Spring, Faulconbridge, & Sarwar, 2022).

Builds onWork redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Open topic: Work redesign: Jagged capability demands selective deployment

Corpus framing of this source: Accommodating Machine Learning Algorithms in Professional Service Firms

Corpus synthesis of this source: Faulconbridge, Sarwar and Spring explain how English accounting and law firms secured professionals’ cooperative adoption of extractive machine learning. They argue that adoption depended on limiting change mainly to lower-valued diagnostic work, preserving human control over inference, treatment and client-facing work, reinventing junior roles, and reallocating technical tasks to technologists. Partnership governance enabled professionals to negotiate and legitimate these changes. The authors qualify this account by showing that professionals’ reassurance about control and algorithmic transparency could be misleading, and by limiting their findings to extractive systems used by early adopters before the generative-AI shift.

Corpus source-specific finding

The study develops an empirically grounded theory of cooperative adoption in professional service firms.

Exact returned source excerpt

Data and methods Between 2018 and 2020 we collected data by studying accounting and law firms in England.

Exact returned source excerpt

We conducted a systematic review of professional and media publications relating to accounting and law and completed interviews with key actors, including from 11 accounting firms and 19 law firms that we identified as being early adopters of machine learning algorithms.

Corpus source-specific finding

The firms used machine learning chiefly as an extractive, human-in-the-loop tool for diagnostic work, not as an autonomous adviser.

Exact returned source excerpt

The packages used in accounting and law were, then, different in that they used machine learn- ing to identify patterns in numerical data in the former and patterns in text in the latter.

Exact returned source excerpt

However, they were also similar in that the purpose of using machine learning was to extract, from very large datasets, relevant sections based on determinations of relevance.

Corpus source-specific finding

Cooperation depended on which categories of professional work changed.

Exact returned source excerpt

This involved positioning the types of diagnosis work that could be completed and changed by machine learning algorithms as less valuable and less meaningful to professionals than inference, treatment and empathy work (see Table 2).

Exact returned source excerpt

As one lawyer put it, ‘Nobody went to law school to then come and read hun- dreds of pages of due diligence documents to extract data to then put it in a spreadsheet for a partner’ (partner, law firm).

Corpus source-specific finding

Adoption reinvented junior work rather than simply eliminating it.

Exact returned source excerpt

For instance, a lawyer described the need for junior professionals to now ‘quality check and sort of review what the [AI] tool has done’ (partner, law firm).

Exact returned source excerpt

This involved checks to see that the text identified for review appeared appropriate accord- ing to the extraction criteria set, while the data visualization described earlier, and statistical repre- sentations of datasets made possible by 100% samples/reviews, created new tasks associated with scrutinizing the insights generated.

Corpus source-specific finding

Firms reallocated infrastructure and system-management work to technologists, producing complementarity but unequal occupational relations.

Exact returned source excerpt

In addition, completely new tasks emerged that were less tied to profession-specific diagnosis work, and more to the computer infrastructures and data repositories needed when using machine learning algorithms.

Exact returned source excerpt

Professionals responded to these new tasks by reallocating work into the domain of a new occupational group – this group being labelled across the firms we studied as ‘technologists’.

Corpus source-specific finding

Partnership governance and professional autonomy enabled negotiated rather than imposed change.

Exact returned source excerpt

It shows that in classic PSFs (von Nordenflycht, 2010) such as accounting and law firms, the use of partnership forms of governance and the autonomy they give professionals are crucial in explain- ing the possibility for cooperative change.

Exact returned source excerpt

Partnerships not only allow professionals to block change as the existing literature suggests, but also allow professionals to retain a sense of control and to identify and legitimize acceptable changes to work when a decision is made to adopt machine learning algorithms.

Corpus source-specific finding

The professionals’ sense of control and risk mitigation could exceed their actual understanding of the technology.

Exact returned source excerpt

This approach to control does not, however, fully account for the way technologists, through their knowledge of machine learning algorithms and decisions about how to manage them, exert agency over how professional work is automated and augmented, given that professionals could not always understand the reasoning or the implications of the decisions made by technologists.

Exact returned source excerpt

But it did lead professionals to feel reassured that they retained control over work (even if this reassurance was potentially misleading).

Corpus source-specific finding

The findings do not establish how firms will respond when generative AI reaches more valued professional tasks.

Exact returned source excerpt

At the time of our data collection, extractive machine learning was the cutting- edge technology, with the step change to generative technologies, illustrated through the public frenzy around ChatGPT in 2023, yet to occur.

Exact returned source excerpt

Generative machine learning algorithms are now capable of automating some tasks associated with inference and treatment work.
