Open topic: Court responsibility: AI requires lifecycle supervision

Corpus framing of this source: Early court AI: cautious experimentation, workforce planning and public accountability

Corpus synthesis of this source: In their Fall 2019 Court Manager article, Marcus W. Reinkensmeyer and Raymond L. Billotte describe limited early court AI deployments and recommend exploring administrative and information-support uses while protecting fairness, transparency and accountability. They distinguish promising examples from fully evaluated implementations, anticipate workforce disruption, and draw on Alan Carlson and AI Now for safeguards.

Corpus source-specific finding

Early deployments offer learning opportunities, not established evidence of effectiveness.

Exact returned source excerpt

Just two years later, with technology advancing at a breakneck pace, we now see some use of AI and robotics in courts, albeit by a small group of early adopters.

Exact returned source excerpt

Admittedly, the initial court-based AI deployments are limited in scope, requiring more time for full-scale implementation and comprehensive evaluation.

Corpus source-specific finding

Courts should explore assistive applications despite objections to high-stakes black-box systems.

Exact returned source excerpt

We believe systems that eliminate or reduce data-entry requirements; support the decision maker in gathering, coordinating, and presenting information; and facilitate real-time information to the public are worthy of serious consideration.

Exact returned source excerpt

We can, and should, explore advanced technology to assist us in providing services to the public that promote efficiency, effectiveness, and accountability.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI may replace court tasks, so leaders should plan organizational transitions before deployment.

Exact returned source excerpt

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus source-specific finding

Algorithmic accountability should include assessment, independent scrutiny and public participation.

Exact returned source excerpt

In addition, we must be diligent in our commitment to accountability and transparency of the algorithms used in our AI systems.

Exact returned source excerpt

In April of 2018, AI Now *published its work, “Algorithmic Impact Assessments: A Practical Framework for Public Agency Accountability.”9 The authors recommend: - agencies should conduct a self-assessment of existing and proposed AI systems, evaluating impacts on fairness, justice, and bias; - agencies should develop a meaningful external researcher review process; - agencies should provide notice to the public disclosing their definition of “automated decision system”; - agencies should solicit public comments to clarify concerns; and - government should provide enhanced due-process
