Builds onAI curriculum: Assistance must preserve intellectual development

Open topic: AI curriculum: Assistance must preserve intellectual development

Corpus framing of this source: Suffolk Law memorandum: AI instruction, learning evidence and institutional safeguards

Corpus synthesis of this source: The memorandum urges Suffolk faculty to revisit current AI capabilities, distinguish better work products from better learning, and review curriculum, assessment and course policies. It combines research findings with comparisons of other law schools, while making its own recommendations conditional on faculty awareness and clear communication.

Corpus source-specific finding

Faculty should reassess current AI tools rather than rely on experience with early or free systems.

Exact returned source excerpt

If your experience with generative AI is limited to an early or free version of ChatGPT, it is worth revisiting one of today’s leading systems using its most capable available setting.

Exact returned source excerpt

In a randomized controlled study involving 137 upper- level law students at the University of Minnesota and the University of Michigan, newer AI tools (using a retrieval-augmented generation platform and an AI reasoning model) improved work-product quality on at least four of six tasks relative to no-AI controls.4 A 2025 industry benchmark similarly reported that an AI product matched or exceeded the report’s lawyer baseline on five of the seven assigned tasks: data extraction, document question answering, document summarization, transcript analysis, and chronology generation.5 Importantly, AI capabilities have improved considerably since these studies were completed.6 Recent research also demonstrates the increasing capability of AI tools in the context of legal education.

Corpus source-specific finding

Widespread student AI use raises both learning and information-governance questions.

Exact returned source excerpt

Exact returned source excerpt

10 Uses can include uploading faculty members’ slides and class recordings to generate outlines or explanations of difficult concepts; generating practice essays and multiple- choice questions and asking AI to evaluate the students’ responses; using AI to simulate Socratic dialogue before class or to answer questions during class; and creating podcasts and other summaries from syllabi, casebook chapters, and slides.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI's effects on legal reasoning depend on how and when students use it.

Exact returned source excerpt

For example, in a 2026 randomized controlled study, ninety-one second- and third-year University of Minnesota law students completed a structured, four-stage exercise designed to test whether students who used AI during an initial phase would show weaker comprehension and reasoning after AI access was removed.11 Students with access to AI produced stronger initial work more quickly, but contrary to the researchers’ hypothesis, their early AI use did not reduce later unaided performance.12 At the revision stage, when all students used AI, students who began with weaker drafts improved while those who began with stronger drafts regressed.13 The study counsels against assuming that AI either inevitably erodes or reliably builds legal reasoning; effects may depend on the task, user, timing, and manner of use.14 Law schools around the country are taking different approaches to this issue.

Exact returned source excerpt
