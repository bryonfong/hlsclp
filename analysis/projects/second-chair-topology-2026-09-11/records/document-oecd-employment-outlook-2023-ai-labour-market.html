Corpus framing of this source: OECD: early AI productivity gains coexist with uncertain employment effects and a continuing need for human oversight

Corpus synthesis of this source: OECD Employment Outlook 2023: Artificial Intelligence and the Labour Market combines its 2022 employer and worker surveys, stakeholder interviews and emerging research. It finds early performance benefits and limited observed employment losses, while stressing narrow evidence, substantial substitution risks and rapid technological change. Corpus framing of this source: OECD: Coordinated, enforceable and adaptive governance for workplace AI

Corpus synthesis of this source: OECD Employment Outlook 2023: Artificial Intelligence and the Labour Market surveys workplace-AI governance as it stood in 2023. It argues for combining existing legal protections, risk-based AI legislation, standards and practical oversight, backed by enforcement capacity and evidence-based review. Its evidence consists chiefly of legislative proposals, policy initiatives and legal examples; it distinguishes promising approaches from demonstrated effectiveness and reports concerns about regulatory gaps and fragmentation.

Corpus source-specific finding

Protecting workers and promoting beneficial AI adoption are complementary policy goals.

Exact returned source excerpt

This means that AI must be safe and respectful of fundamental rights such as privacy and fairness, the right of labour to organise, transparency and explainability.

Exact returned source excerpt

Proactive and decisive action is not only important to protect workers, but also to promote AI innovation and diffusion because it reduces uncertainty.

Corpus source-specific finding

Existing law provides a foundation, but its adequacy for workplace AI remains uncertain.

Exact returned source excerpt

• Existing (non-AI specific) legislation – for instance on discrimination, workers’ rights to organise, or product liability – is an important foundation for regulating workplace AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

For instance, all OECD member countries have in place laws that aim to protect data and privacy.

Corpus source-specific finding

The proposed EU AI Act illustrates differentiated regulation of employment risks.

Exact returned source excerpt

The proposed act follows a risk-based regulatory approach that differentiates between uses of AI that generate: i) minimal risk; ii) low risk; iii) high risk; and iv) unacceptable risk.

Exact returned source excerpt

It classifies certain AI systems used for recruitment, decisions about promotion, firing and task assignment, and monitoring of persons in work-related contractual relationships as “high risk”.

Corpus source-specific finding

Reviews and regulatory sandboxes can support adaptation and regulatory learning.

Exact returned source excerpt

To minimise the regulatory burden and mitigate the risk that AI legislation cannot keep up with such a fast-changing technology, legislative proposals use measures differentiated typically by risk category – with regular reviews of such categories – and regulatory sandboxes.

Exact returned source excerpt

To address the main issues that raise legal uncertainty for providers and to contribute to evidence-based regulatory learning, the act encourages EU member states to establish “AI regulatory sandboxes”, where AI systems can be developed and tested in a controlled environment in the pre-marketing phase.

Corpus source-specific finding

Countries are developing explanation, human-oversight and auditing measures, but experts question the pace of policy.

Exact returned source excerpt

In the United States in October 2022, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy published a Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, which laid out a roadmap for the responsible use of AI.

Exact returned source excerpt

In June 2022, Canada introduced in Parliament the Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA) which requires “plain language explanations” of how AI systems reach their outcomes.

Corpus source-specific finding

Soft law and technical tools support trustworthy AI but cannot substitute reliably for enforceable protections.

Exact returned source excerpt

Principles like those developed by the OECD can help promote the use of AI that is innovative and trustworthy and that respects human rights and democratic values (OECD, 2019[22]).

Exact returned source excerpt

For example, Australia’s Digital Transformation Agency developed a guide to AI adoption in the public sector, stipulating, amongst others, that human decision-makers remain responsible for decisions assisted by machines, and that they must therefore understand the inputs and outputs of the technologies (Australian Government, 2023[17]).

Corpus source-specific finding

Policy coordination must limit inconsistency and compliance costs.

Exact returned source excerpt

While using different measures at the same time can help address gaps in AI policies, this approach poses challenges in terms of possible regulatory burdens or inconsistent policies.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

The success of these measures will depend as much on their formulation as on their implementation.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus framing of this source: EU proposals to adapt liability rules to AI-related harm

Corpus synthesis of this source: The OECD Employment Outlook 2023 describes the European Commission’s September 2022 liability proposals and their rationale. It distinguishes strict product liability from fault-based AI liability and reports intended protections for victims, rather than offering an independent recommendation or evidence of implementation outcomes.

Corpus source-specific finding

The Commission proposed two complementary routes to harmonise national AI liability rules.

Exact returned source excerpt

Additionally, in September 2022 the European Commission proposed a targeted harmonisation of national liability rules for AI through two proposals: a review of the Directive on liability for defective products – Product Liability Directive for short – and a proposal for a Directive on adapting non contractual civil liability rules to artificial intelligence – AI Liability Directive for short – (European Commission, 2022[126]).

Corpus source-specific finding

The product-liability revision would retain strict manufacturer liability while clarifying compensation for AI-related defective products.

Exact returned source excerpt

Current product liability rules in the EU are based on the strict liability of manufacturers, meaning that when a defective product causes harm, the product’s manufacturer must pay damages without the need for the claimant to establish the manufacturer’s fault or negligence.

Exact returned source excerpt

The revised Product Liability Directive modernises and reinforces existing product liability rules to provide legal clarity to businesses regarding fair compensation to victims of defective products that involve AI, but will maintain the strict liability regime.

Corpus source-specific finding

AI’s opacity and complexity motivate a proposed easing of proof requirements under fault-based liability.

Exact returned source excerpt

In contrast to strict liability, fault-based liability regimes put the burden of proof on the claimant, who must demonstrate that the party being accused of wrongdoing (such as the manufacturer) failed to meet the standard of care expected in a given situation (Goldberg and Zipursky, 2016[127]).

Exact returned source excerpt

However, the opacity and complexity of AI systems can make it difficult and expensive for victims to build cases and explain in detail how harm was caused.

Corpus source-specific finding

The proposals would address fundamental-rights harms and improve access to algorithmic information.

Exact returned source excerpt

To address these difficulties, the AI Liability Directive – a fault-based liability regime – proposes to alleviate the burden of proof for AI victims through a so-called rebuttable “presumption of causality” (European Commission, 2022[126]).31 The Directive also expands the definition of “harm”, from health and safety issues to infringements on fundamental rights as well (including discrimination and breaches of privacy).

Exact returned source excerpt

Furthermore, together with the revised Product Liability Directive, it aims to facilitate easier access to information about the algorithms for European courts and individuals (Goujard, 2022[128]).32

Corpus synthesis of this source: The OECD Employment Outlook 2023 combines a task-based economic framework, published studies and new employer and worker research. It finds limited aggregate employment effects so far, alongside changes in productivity, job quality and skill demand. It distinguishes AI exposure from automation risk, treats early generative-AI findings cautiously, and recommends training, worker participation and trustworthy-AI governance.
