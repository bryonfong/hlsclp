Corpus framing of this source: How automation displaces labor and new tasks reinstate it

Corpus synthesis of this source: Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor develops a task-based account of technological change, applies it to US labor-demand trends, and argues that outcomes depend on the balance between automation, new labor-intensive tasks and productivity improvements. Its conclusions concern the wage bill, not a separately estimated employment forecast.

Corpus framing of this source: Automation displaces labor; new labor-intensive tasks can restore demand

Corpus synthesis of this source: In their March 2019 paper, Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo develop a task-based account of technological change and apply it to postwar US labor demand. Their decomposition attributes recent weakness to stronger displacement, weaker creation of new tasks and slower productivity growth. They make the future of work conditional on the mix of technologies rather than predicting inevitable joblessness or automatic gains for workers.

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

New technologies not only increase the productivity of capital and labor at tasks they currently perform, but also impact the allocation of tasks to these factors of production—what we call the task content of production.

Exact returned source excerpt

These technologies are qualitatively different from factor-augmenting ones which do not impact the task content of production.

Corpus source-specific finding

New labor-intensive tasks counterbalance displacement through a reinstatement effect.

Exact returned source excerpt

Throughout our focus is on “labor-intensive” new tasks, and for brevity, we will simply refer to these as “new tasks”.

Exact returned source excerpt

The introduction of new tasks changes the task content of production in favor of labor because of a reinstatement effect, and always raises the labor share and labor demand.

Corpus source-specific finding

Exact returned source excerpt

In the second part of the paper, we use our framework to study the evolution of labor demand in the United States since World War II and explain how industry data can be used to infer the behavior of the task content of production and the displacement and reinstatement effects.

Exact returned source excerpt

We establish this by studying the evolution of the economy-wide wage bill, which combines information on average wages and total employment and is thus informative about changes in overall labor demand.

The decomposition attributes the recent labor-demand slowdown to stronger displacement, weaker reinstatement and weak productivity growth.

Exact returned source excerpt

Corpus framing of this source: Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor

Corpus synthesis of this source: Acemoglu and Restrepo develop a task-based framework that separates automation’s productivity and displacement effects from the reinstatement effect of new labor-intensive tasks. They use industry evidence and a wage-bill decomposition to argue that accelerated automation, insufficient new-task creation and weak productivity growth explain the post-1987 slowdown in US labor-demand growth, while identifying important assumptions and possible confounders.
