What this shows+

Twenty-seven forces acting on legal work, and the seventy-six ways they act on each other. Each driver is a named mechanism rather than a topic, and they are grouped by the layer of legal production they bite on — from what machines can attempt through to who gets served. That grouping is the argument: a driver is placed by where it acts, not by which category it belongs to. Ten feedback loops run through them, and a loop is only ever as well evidenced as the weakest driver on it.

How to read it+
Discs are drivers — a named force with a stated mechanism, not a topic. Size follows how many other drivers each one touches; the outline says which part of legal production it acts on (how work is made, how it is checked, how it is paid for, who is allowed to do it).
Arrows say how one driver acts on another: amplifying (makes it stronger), dampening (holds it back), or conditional (only under stated circumstances). Heavier arrows are amplifying links held with high confidence.

What it is built from+

Second Chair is this project’s evidence base: 600 works on AI and the legal profession, 1953–2026 — scholarship, industry evidence, court materials and practitioner interviews — each read end to end. The Analysis section applies two published analytical lenses: topic mapping and actor mapping, with driver and dynamics mapping and scenario mapping in progress.
